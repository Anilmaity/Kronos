## Increase Risk to Reward Ratio Using Lower Time Frames (`FXNLGYxfbDc`, https://youtu.be/FXNLGYxfbDc, 178s)

### What is actually taught

A three-minute arithmetic demonstration of one idea, worked on a single setup with a fixed
target (the previous day's low). The higher-timeframe point of interest is an hourly fair
value gap; the question is only where to execute. On the 15-minute there is no way to lower
the entry, so you fill on the gap, stop above the high, and get **1.79R** — "which isn't
horrible but can be better". Dropping to the 5-minute produces an order block at the same
place and therefore the *same* ratio; the honest inclusion of a refinement that yields nothing
is the useful part. On the 1-minute he waits for a **confirmed** setup — a structure break plus
a fair value gap — which allows a materially tighter stop against the same target.

The payoff is stated in win-rate terms rather than profit terms, which is unusual for this
channel: a higher risk-to-reward "allows you to have a lower win rate". He also flags a stop
choice inside the 5-minute case — you can risk the order block's own low or the lower low
beneath it, and since the nearer one "does have a chance of being ran", the wider is "safer".

### Rules stated

- Fix the target first; refine only the entry.
- Compute R at the current timeframe, then step down: 15m → 5m → 1m.
- A step down may produce nothing; step again.
- On the lowest timeframe wait for a *confirmed* setup, not the raw level.
- Where two stops are available and the nearer can be run, take the wider one.

### Open questions

- No minimum acceptable ratio is stated. The 2R minimum that saturates the later material is
  entirely absent; 1.79R is merely "not horrible".
- No stopping rule for how far down to refine. (Other videos in this unit go to 15-second and
  even 5-second charts.)
- The lower-win-rate claim is asserted, never computed.

## Intraday Bias (`xdm_OexQWJY`, https://youtu.be/xdm_OexQWJY, 205s)

### What is actually taught

**This is a different intraday-bias procedure from the canonical one**, and the difference is
the most consequential evolution finding in the unit after displacement. The frame is stated
in one line: use the **4-hour and 1-hour charts as your daily timeframe** and base trades off
them. The bias then comes from a closed list of exactly three inputs — **displacement,
structure, and imbalances**. In the first example: an aggressive move down (displacement),
relatively equal lows beneath it (structure), and the gap the move left behind (imbalance).
Those three together say where price wants to go; only then do you drop to a lower timeframe
and look for the entry — a retracement into an OTE after liquidity is run, targeting the lows.

The second half adds a separate construction he calls framing the *narrative*: read London and
Asia's price action together with the previous session's. The worked chain is a previous high
run, displacement down leaving a fair value gap, London running highs back up into that gap and
forming an intermediate-term high; if that high holds, the read is bearish and the draw is the
trendline/resting liquidity beneath the large gaps below. He then runs the mirror — lows run,
then displacement up, so look back long.

The later canonical intraday-bias video (`6zTz7WMdMWg`) does none of this. It derives bias from
the **previous 4-hour candle's high and low** as a continuous draw engine — take one side and
close outside means continuation, close back inside means reversal. That mechanism does not
appear here at all, and the three-input version does not appear there.

### Rules stated

- Work on 4H and 1H as if they were the daily.
- Bias = displacement + structure + imbalance, read together.
- Preferred sequence: old lows run → displacement up → look for longs on the lower timeframe.
- Target the obvious liquidity, or a fair value gap in a premium (bearish: discount).
- Alternative framing: London/Asia price action against the previous session's.

### Open questions

- No precedence when the three inputs disagree.
- "Displacement" unquantified (see the displacement video below).
- The London/Asia framing gives no session boundaries.

## Intro to Market Structure Shifts, Fair Value Gaps, and Displacement (`PePk1V0Q4QQ`, https://youtu.be/PePk1V0Q4QQ, 245s)

### What is actually taught

The foundation lesson, and its value now is that it shows how lean the original definitions
were. Market structure is nothing more than highs and lows — "short term highs and lows swing
highs and lows relative equal highs and lows" — and the reason to care is that liquidity rests
beyond them. A market structure shift "will show you when the trend is changing": in a
downtrend you watch the successive lower highs, and the shift is one of them being **broken
with displacement**. That is the entire test. No stop raid, no prior engagement with a
higher-timeframe level.

Displacement is then defined *because MSS needs it*: "an energetic move up that breaks this
structure". And then the line that matters for the whole corpus — **"for displacement to occur
you want to see a fair value gap created"**. In this recording the gap is a *requirement*. In
the later canon (`_94CPMjWi9E`) it has been demoted to a detection aid: "the easiest way to
spot it is just looking for fair value gaps". He also gives the reason for the requirement, and
it is a distance argument: the gap "shows that price moved rather quickly through this area…
you want to see price reaching above this area quickly before retracing".

The fair value gap itself is defined on three candles and on the **wicks**: candles 1 and 3
normally have overlapping wicks ("price was delivered well there"), and where they do not
overlap you have "a break in price delivery". That gap is the entry.

### Rules stated

- MSS = a prior high/low broken with displacement. In a downtrend, watch the highs.
- Displacement = energetic move that breaks structure **and creates a fair value gap**.
- FVG = three candles, wick of 1 not overlapping wick of 3.
- The gap left by the displacing leg is the entry.

### Open questions

- Whether the gap must be left by the breaking candle itself or anywhere in the leg.
- "Energetic" carries no threshold here; the measurement procedure only arrives in `1oco9lesido`.

## Intro to the Inducement Model (`k3hTgbHBz1U`, https://youtu.be/k3hTgbHBz1U, 186s)

### What is actually taught

Not a definition — a **five-step model**, presented explicitly as a comparison to "the 2022
mentorship model". The bearish sequence: a high is put in with liquidity resting above; that
liquidity is run; structure breaks; internal range liquidity forms inside the resulting range;
that internal liquidity is run **before the real move begins**. The bullish walk-through is
mirrored and more explicit: a low, then a lower high, then the low is run "sweeping the
liquidity grabbing it", then a high breaking structure, then "a low that induces people to go
long" — and that low is what gets run before the actual move.

The mechanism he names is stop placement: participants enter at the induced level and rest
their stops just beyond it, *including* those who moved to break-even after a favourable move.
His preference is for a fair value gap to sit at the inducement — the five-minute gap in the
first example, an hourly one in the higher-timeframe example. His own one-line compression is
the definition to keep: "really it's just internal liquidity in a range that is ran before the
actual move".

The later `Inducement` video (`-GkOTMYoJT4`) reduces all of this to a general statement — "a
move that induces buyers or sellers into the market" — plus a preference for the inducement to
occur inside a PD array. The five-step sequence is not restated there.

### Rules stated

- Mark the external extreme; wait for it to be run.
- Structure breaks in the intended direction.
- Identify the internal range liquidity created inside the new range — prefer a fair value gap there.
- Wait for that internal level to be run; only then does the real move begin.

### Open questions

- The video's title promises the comparison to the 2022 mentorship model and the transcript
  never delivers it — he says it is "similar and different" and moves on.
- Which internal swing is *the* inducement when several exist is not stated.
- No timing bound between the structure break and the run of the internal level.

## Low Resistance Liquidity Run (`2lIaRUtJqpU`, https://youtu.be/2lIaRUtJqpU, 545s)

### What is actually taught

**The dedicated definitional lesson for a pair the library previously held only in circular,
regime-level form**, and the definition it gives is fully decidable in one sentence: "low
resistance liquidity is just a high or low with a failure swing while high resistance liquidity
does not have a failure swing and price sweeps the liquidity before reversing". Concretely: mark
a level, watch the next price leg toward it; if the leg turned *before* reaching it, the
liquidity there is untaken and the area is low resistance; if the leg took it out and then
reversed, the stops there are gone and what remains is the liquidity at the next extreme.

The use is a target filter applied **before the session**: he marks the low-resistance side and
targets it, because price is much more likely to expand through untaken liquidity. Two worked
NASDAQ 15-minute cases, one GBPUSD daily case, and a final NASDAQ 5m/1m case all do the same
thing — locate the failure-swing side and let price run through it.

Two mechanisms in the video are more valuable than the definition itself:

**The daily scan.** "another thing I will look for are consecutive candles where no high is
broken" — runs of consecutive daily candles in which no previous day high was ever taken out.
Drop to the 4-hour and every one of those previous day highs is sitting there unswept, so the
whole band is low resistance; if price starts breaking through, expect it to continue and
expand in that direction. That is a screen you could write today.

**The high-resistance target substitution.** When the level you would otherwise target has
already been swept (in his example price took the low, closed back inside, and the *next*
candle opened and closed back inside as well), he does not use it. He substitutes either the
**failure swing** that formed just after it, or the **consequent encroachment of the wick** left
by the close back into the range — "this is going to be a lot harder to break through than this
low resistance liquidity right here". The closing example makes it a time argument as well as a
probability one: he would "rather save myself the time and just exit my position at this failure
swing and equal low" than hold for the distant swept low, and the chart shows exactly that
consolidation happening.

### Rules stated

- Low resistance = a high/low with a failure swing in front of it. High resistance = one that was
  swept before the reversal.
- Mark the low-resistance side before the session; that is the target.
- Daily scan: consecutive days with no previous day high taken ⇒ a low-resistance band.
- Do not target a high-resistance extreme directly; substitute the failure swing or the wick CE.
- Between two correlated assets, prefer the one with low resistance liquidity in your direction.

### Open questions

- "The leg toward it" is not bounded in candles or time, so the classification depends on an
  unstated lookback.
- Whether a wick through the level (rather than a close) converts low resistance to high
  resistance is not stated.
- The consecutive-days scan has no minimum count.
- No rule chooses between the failure swing and the wick CE when both are available.

## Lower Time Frame Entries Using HTF POI (`YmdC_LKT-Pk`, https://youtu.be/YmdC_LKT-Pk, 570s)

### What is actually taught

A session-review video rather than a lesson, and it is the most candid in the unit — the whole
back half is him showing a trade he took **against his own bias** and explaining why that was
fine. Structurally it is the higher-timeframe-POI-then-lower-timeframe-entry procedure
(a 4-hour fair value gap / BPR is the POI; return to it, then hunt the entry). Two things in it
are new to the library.

**The re-anchoring ladder.** After displacement, "draw out your OTE from where your displacement
range started to where it ended", then look for a PD array in the discount. He then misses the
fill four consecutive times, and each time the procedure is identical: wait for the next
displacement leg, **move the anchors to that leg**, look again in the new discount — and note
that each iteration also leaves fresh internal liquidity that he wants swept before the
continuation. The stopping condition is stated: "you can just keep doing this until you reach
your objective". This is a complete, loopable recovery procedure for a missed entry and I have
not seen it stated anywhere else in the corpus.

**Failure to displace as a bias flip, executed live.** He was short-biased above the highs.
Price failed to close or displace below a one-minute low — "we can't close or displace flow
there no fair value gaps" — and he took the *long*: "that's because failure to displace lower
popped up there is our entry long". He explicitly defers the mechanics to the following week's
video, which is `5rbFskdmEmU` below.

Two smaller items. He uses SMT for **asset selection**, not signalling: NASDAQ could not take
the lows that ES took, "so to me that pointed out that NASDAQ was a little bit stronger", and he
took the long on NASDAQ. And he identifies a 15-second box setup by shape alone — "aggressive
down aggressive up does that remind you of the box or BPR" — treating box setup and BPR as the
same geometry. He drops to a **5-second chart** to show it, the lowest timeframe anywhere in the
corpus.

He also states the negative rule that gates the whole procedure: "I don't just short when we're
above those highs I wait for a model".

### Rules stated

- Mark the HTF POI; wait for the return; only then look for a lower-timeframe model.
- Being at a level you have an opinion about is not an entry.
- Anchor the OTE across the displacement range; re-anchor to each new leg after a miss.
- Expect the internal liquidity created by each leg to be swept before the continuation.
- A failure to displace against the position is a reason to flip, not to fade.
- Trade the stronger correlated asset for longs, the weaker for shorts.

### Open questions

- No cap on re-anchor iterations, and no rule for abandoning the objective.
- Whether the old range is discarded or kept alongside the new one.
- The 5-second usage is demonstrated, never justified.

## OTE Optimal Trade Entry (`gZLLuB_rmis`, https://youtu.be/gZLLuB_rmis, 188s)

### What is actually taught

Corroborating and slightly narrower than the later canon. Anchor the fib across the displacement
range; the 0.5 marks premium/discount and the retracement is expected "into this area around the
0.62 0.79". Confluence with an imbalance or point of interest inside the band is stated as
helpful — "it does help if you have some sort of imbalance or point of interest" — not required.

Two uses beyond the obvious. First, **nesting**: after entering an OTE band, a new structure
break inside it defines a tighter one, "so you have an optimal trade entry inside of an optimal
trade entry". Second, the same fib levels are used as **targets** when positioned the other way —
if you got short at the high of the leg, the discount fib levels are where you take profit.

**Notably absent: the 0.705 midpoint preference** recorded from `the_foundation_01`. This
recording names only 0.62 and 0.79.

- Anchor from the start to the end of the displacement range; 0.5 = premium/discount boundary.
- OTE band = 0.62 to 0.79.
- Prefer a PD array inside the band.
- Nest: a new leg inside the band gives a tighter OTE.
- Inverse use: the band is a target when positioned against the leg.

### Open questions

- No rule chooses between 0.62 and 0.79 as the order price.
- No invalidation for the OTE itself.
- "five-minute parabola" appears once as a confluence and is not recoverable from the captions.

## Open High Low Close (OHLC / OLHC) (`YXoNowXQirM`, https://youtu.be/YXoNowXQirM, 331s)

### What is actually taught

**This is the dedicated video the library's `daily-ohlc-candle-shape` entry records as missing**
("the speaker explicitly defers the definition to a separate 'Open High Low Close' video that is
not in this unit"). It is here, and the argument turns out to be arithmetic rather than
behavioural: every candle has an open, a high, a low and a close, so unless a bullish candle
opens exactly on its low it **must** trade below its open to put the low in before expanding —
open, low, high, close. A bearish candle must put the high in above the open first.

The trading consequence is positional and is stated twice: with a bullish expectation, "look for
opportunities to buy below the open or near the open"; with a bearish one, "look for selling
opportunities between this high and around this opening price". Which shape you expect is
supplied entirely from outside — "this is dependent on my draw on liquidity".

It is demonstrated at three scales: the **weekly** candle read from daily candles (Monday's low,
then expansion days, then the close); the **daily** candle read on a 15-minute chart; and the
**4-hour** candle read on a 5-minute chart, where he adds a live confirmation test — "that
opening price is supporting the candles and nothing is closing below it". He closes by mapping
the same shape onto AMD, and the mapping is anchored on the **opening price** rather than on an
arbitrary consolidation box: consolidation around the open is accumulation, "what happens when
we go above this opening price that is our manipulation", and the sell-off to the low and close
is distribution.

**The conflict.** For the daily candle he says outright: "I am going to be using midnight opening
price as the open for this daily candle." The later canon anchors the daily candle at **18:00**,
and the direct Q&A answer recorded in the library is that he *always* uses 18:00 and does not use
the midnight or True Day open. This is a change of practice, and it is recorded as such in
`daily-open-eighteen-hundred`.

### Rules stated

- Bullish expectation → open, low, high, close. Position at or below the open.
- Bearish expectation → open, high, low, close. Position between the high and the open.
- The draw on liquidity selects the shape.
- Live confirmation: the opening price should support the lower-timeframe candles.
- AMD anchored on the open: consolidation = accumulation, excursion beyond = manipulation,
  move to the far extreme = distribution.

### Open questions

- No close-location or body-to-range threshold for calling a candle directional.
- The rule is about the *order* of the extremes, so it is only decidable while the candle forms.
- No tolerance for "near the open".
- The midnight-vs-18:00 daily open is unreconciled across the corpus.

## Price Targets (`pWAjzKndWXo`, https://youtu.be/pWAjzKndWXo, 235s)

### What is actually taught

A closed two-item list: "the two things i use to choose targets is liquidity and imbalances".
Liquidity means the lows and highs where stops rest, and relatively equal lows/highs are
singled out as unconditional — "whenever you see equal lows that should be a target in your
mind". Imbalances means fair value gaps, both on the trading timeframe and one timeframe up
(he cites a 5-minute gap and the 15-minute gap that coincides with the same liquidity run), plus
order blocks used the same way.

The reason he gives for adding imbalances is a trade-off, stated plainly: "you might exit a
little bit more early but it also will give you a few more targets". And the corollary he
demonstrates is **counter-trend scalping inside a larger move** — chain the gaps, "target
imbalance to imbalance to imbalance", with the structural high as the eventual real objective.
He is careful to flag the example he would not have taken ("if you get short in this order block
up here which i probably wouldn't"), which keeps the video honest.

### Rules stated

- Targets = liquidity (stops at highs/lows; equal highs/lows always) and imbalances (FVGs on the
  trading timeframe and above; order blocks).
- Prefer a level where both coincide.
- Counter-trend: take the nearest imbalance, then the next.
- Accept earlier exits in exchange for more targets.

### Open questions

- No rule orders competing targets in the path.
- "Equal lows" has no tolerance.
- No minimum distance for a target to be worth taking.

## Refine Entries and Scalp Using the 15 Second Chart (`ivirRu3fc4o`, https://youtu.be/ivirRu3fc4o, 356s)

### What is actually taught

Three separate uses of sub-minute charts, and the first is the most mechanical thing in the unit
after the displacement measurement.

**Nesting.** Having marked the 15-minute fair value gap sitting in the OTE of a displacement
range, he drops to the 5-minute and marks its gap, then drops to the 15-second and looks *left*
for a gap "nested in between the 15 minute and five minute fair value gap". That innermost gap
is the entry, and the stated benefit is not a better ratio but **"little to no drawdown"** — the
position is taken at the tightest point of a three-timeframe stack.

**Confirmation.** If the nested entry is missed, wait for the move out of the area, let relative
equal highs form as the new target, mark the OTE of that leg and take a 1-minute order block /
fair value gap entry into it, then either move to break-even or hold for the higher-timeframe
15-second gap above.

**Scalping.** The third use is a standalone model and he states it does not require a narrative:
"you don't necessarily need a higher time frame narrative to scalp these quick moves. it does
help". The shape is a *counter-trend* one — after an aggressive move up stalls, "take a counter
trend scalp down into the ote to then target the other direction". The counter-trend leg needs
its own mini-model (aggressive move down breaking structure, a fair value gap, a return to it);
enter at the start of the gap, stop on the high just above, and exit at the discount / 0.62 of
the leg. He states the temperament this is built around — "i prefer to be wrong or write [right]
pretty quickly" — and takes partials in the OTE region rather than the full move.

### Rules stated

- Nest fair value gaps across three timeframes; enter at the innermost.
- If missed, wait for the move out and take the confirmation entry at the new leg's OTE.
- Counter-trend scalp: stalled leg → aggressive opposing move breaking structure → FVG → return.
- Stop just beyond the gap candle; target the 0.62 / discount; take partials there.

### Open questions

- "Look to the left" for the nested gap has no lookback bound.
- No rule for when the lower-timeframe gap sits outside the higher one.
- No session or time filter is applied to the scalps, and the model sits badly against the
  overtrading and one-trade-per-day discipline recorded elsewhere in the corpus.

## Reversal or Pullback? Using Market Structure To Determine Trend (`FdRKBTz0Fps`, https://youtu.be/FdRKBTz0Fps, 270s)

### What is actually taught

One decidable test plus a timeframe hierarchy. The test: mark the short-term lows in an uptrend
(highs in a downtrend) and watch whether they are broken with displacement. **"if price does not
break structure it's just a pullback."** He then walks a chart marking lows "not broken, not
broken" until one breaks.

The hierarchy is the substance. He reads trend on the 5-minute but says the 15-minute governs
and is what he recommends to most people — "less noise and it's a little more reliable". So when
the 5-minute turns bearish underneath an intact 15-minute uptrend, that is not a reversal: it is
a counter-trend leg to be traded *to* the 15-minute imbalance, where longs are reloaded. And
re-entry long at that point of interest requires its own 5-minute or 1-minute structure shift —
he takes the 5-minute one only reluctantly ("didn't really give us a whole lot") and prefers the
1-minute version after more retracement.

The 15-minute section supplies the negative case that makes the displacement requirement real.
A short-term high *was* broken, and he rejects it: "there's no displacement right there's no
fair value gap or reach above energetically". He re-marks the highs and only accepts the later
break that leaves an imbalance.

He ends on the overnight session, "which i don't really mess with" — one of the three session
statements in the unit.

### Rules stated

- Structure not broken ⇒ pullback. Broken with displacement ⇒ reversal on that timeframe.
- A break with no displacement and no fair value gap does not count.
- Check the timeframe above; an intact HTF structure demotes the LTF reversal to a retracement.
- Reload at the HTF imbalance; require a 5m or 1m structure shift to re-enter.
- Recommended analysis timeframe: 15-minute.

### Open questions

- Only the 5m/15m pair is given; other pairings are implied by fractality, not stated.
- "Short-term low" is not defined by a candle count here.

## SMT Divergence (`q1NmxUTm4n4`, https://youtu.be/q1NmxUTm4n4, 548s)

### What is actually taught

The longer of the unit's two SMT lessons, and the only punctuated transcript in it. The
definition is built up structurally — correlated assets normally print the same structure, lower
low against lower low; an SMT is where one diverges, e.g. a higher low against a lower low — and
then **reduced to its minimal decidable form**, which is the sentence worth keeping: "We have a
certain high we are watching. One asset does not break it. One asset does break it. That is our
structure that aligns to give us our SMT."

An SMT at the lows is bullish, at the highs bearish. He works four identifications (S&P vs Dow
at highs, NASDAQ vs S&P at a 2:00 a.m. low, then a deliberately blank chart with a pause for the
viewer) before moving to usage.

**A third SMT location appears here in his own voice**, which the library previously held only
from a guest: the **SMT inside a fair value gap**. After an aggressive move both assets leave a
gap; the divergence is that S&P trades up into its gap while NASDAQ does not. "So, we have an
SMT within a fair value gap."

Usage is stated as confluence only: "I personally use SMTs as a confluence to an already
existing model." When one forms, he drops to a lower timeframe and takes his ordinary entry —
the deviation retest / box setup at a BPR, or a structure break plus fair value gap, or an order
block.

Two secondary items are individually valuable. **Cross-asset target transfer**, in his own voice:
he holds an ES short and exits it at the moment NASDAQ tags *its* lows at 8:35, creating the SMT —
"sometimes I will use Nasdaq hitting price targets as my price target for ES". And **asset
selection**, which he leaves discretionary: which index he trades "is completely dependent on the
lower time frames and which one looks cleaner to me", though he then applies a decidable
criterion anyway — on the move back up he would trade NASDAQ "as it has low resistance liquidity
above".

### Rules stated

- SMT = one shared level; one asset breaks it, the other does not.
- Lows ⇒ bullish; highs ⇒ bearish.
- The same test applies at a shared fair value gap.
- SMT is confluence; take the entry from an existing lower-timeframe model.
- Exit on the correlated asset's level when it is the one that matters.
- Pick the asset by lower-timeframe cleanliness, and by which side carries low resistance liquidity.

### Open questions

- No comparison window: "a certain high we are watching" is chosen visually.
- No tolerance for exactly equal highs/lows.
- The correlated set is never enumerated beyond ES, NQ, YM.
- "Trades into the gap" is not specified as near edge, CE or full fill.

## Seek & Destroy Profile (`PlZD45uLPjE`, https://youtu.be/PlZD45uLPjE, 596s)

### What is actually taught

The dedicated earlier lesson, and it supplies the piece the library explicitly recorded as
missing — *how to trade the profile*.

The definition is causal rather than descriptive: a seek and destroy profile is "a lack of higher
time frame Direction relative to the time frame you're watching which leaves us with
consolidation". The behavioural consequence is that every attempt to leave the range returns into
it — buyside taken then back inside, sellside taken then back inside. It is explicitly fractal:
"this concept is fractal", and smaller seek-and-destroy profiles nest inside larger ones. His
default is avoidance: "generally I like to stay away from this profile as it's pretty choppy".

If he does trade it, the procedure is entirely built on **deviations**: "the first thing I do is
identify deviations from the range" — every point where price took buyside or sellside and came
back in. Those are the only trade locations. And then **the target ladder, numbered**: "there are
three things that I look for that is one equilibrium two OTE and then three the other side of the
range buy side or sell side liquidity". He applies exactly that ladder to every deviation walked
in the video, in both directions, and notes that price in this profile "will respect that fairly
well" — the fib retracement levels hold.

The daily anticipation trigger is session-based: "I look for a lemon [London] session to take the
high and the low of the Asian range", which creates a deviation on both sides, after which New
York a.m. is expected either to consolidate or to target London's own high and low. Two long
walkthroughs follow, one on the Asian range and one using a larger consolidation range with the
Asia high/low nested inside it — the second is where the fractality is demonstrated most clearly,
with each smaller sweep-and-return getting the same three-target treatment.

The later `Broadening Formations — ICT Seek & Destroy` video (`Tjk9bXERZy0`) anticipates the
profile from an **empty economic calendar** instead. News is not mentioned once here.

### Rules stated

- Definition: lack of HTF direction relative to the traded timeframe ⇒ consolidation.
- Mark every deviation (side taken, close back inside); trade only from those.
- Targets in order: equilibrium of the range, the OTE, the opposite side's liquidity.
- Daily trigger: London takes both Asia extremes ⇒ NY a.m. consolidates or targets London's extremes.
- Treat the profile as fractal.
- Default is to stand aside.

### Open questions

- "Lack of higher timeframe direction" has no test.
- Which range anchors the equilibrium is not fixed — the outer consolidation and the individual
  price leg are both used.
- No deadline for London taking both Asia extremes.
- No rule on scaling out across the three rungs versus picking one.

### What is actually taught

The shorter SMT video, and **it disagrees with the longer one in the same playlist on two

**Asset selection is mechanical here, not discretionary.** "you want to take the one that is

- Direct entry: stop at the diverging low/high; if taken, the SMT is invalid.

## Stop Losses, Placement & Trailing (`LZZAaILYNyI`, https://youtu.be/LZZAaILYNyI, 283s)

### What is actually taught

Framed from the first sentence as "a template for you to work on… and to experiment with", not
as a rule, and that framing should be carried into any use of it.

**Placement**: two options only. The high (short) or low (long) of the **candle that made** the
fair value gap or order block being entered, or the **short-term high/low** containing it. He
switches between them "depending on my risk tolerance for that certain play", and the tie-break
he actually applies is what sits beyond the level — imbalances above push him wider.

**Trailing** is where the video earns its place, because it states a *gate* that the later
material states differently. Two conditions, both required: he has taken his **first partial**,
and **structure has broken**. "you're going to want to only move your stop once structure is
broken." On the break the stop goes to the entry price or to the short-term high the break has
just protected — "as it should be protected" — and the procedure repeats on every subsequent
break. He shows one case where the drop to the new extreme is very large and says take partials
instead of relying on the trail alone, and one where the last structure was never broken so the
short-term high is used once price begins shifting against the position. Tighter trailing on the
1-minute is permitted with the same gate: "just make sure you get a shift in structure before you
move your stop".

The later canon states the same geometry in **protected-swing** vocabulary and adds a condition
this version does not have: the trail target must have engaged a point of interest and then been
closed through. Here, any short-term high after a structure break qualifies.

### Rules stated

- Placement: the gap/block candle's extreme, or the short-term high/low. Choose by risk tolerance
  and by what sits beyond.
- Do not move the stop until a first partial is taken and structure breaks.
- Move to the entry price or the newly protected short-term extreme; repeat each break.
- Where the jump is large, take partials rather than only trailing.
- Tighter trailing on the 1-minute, same gate.

### Open questions

- "Break structure" is given no displacement requirement here, unlike in the MSS lesson.
- No buffer beyond the extreme (ticks, points, ATR).
- The first-partial precondition does not appear in the later canon.

## Structure Shift vs Liquidity Grab (`5rbFskdmEmU`, https://youtu.be/5rbFskdmEmU, 480s)

### What is actually taught

The title is misleading; the video's actual subject, stated in the first sentence, is
"displacement and the lack of displacement", and it is the promised follow-up to the bias flip
in `YmdC_LKT-Pk`. It contributes two things of first-rank importance.

**A displacement definition with a shape test.** "displacement is just large aggressive candles
with closes in the high or the low such as here" — the close sits *at* the candle's extreme,
which is the nearest the corpus ever gets to a wick-size rule — "but you want to see it over
structure… you notice these aggressive candles break structure up that is displacement over
structure". Two conditions: candle shape, and location relative to a structural level.

**The lack-of-displacement doctrine**, which he names as the most important takeaway and which
inverts the usual emphasis. Rather than waiting for displacement in your direction, use the
*failure* to displace against you as the entry, gated by which side of the curve you are on: "if
you're trying to get into a trend on a buy side of the curve you want to see lows taken without
displacement and that can give you entries to get long and then when you're on the sell side of
the curve when you fail to displace over highs that can be your short entries". The general form
is stated as a symmetry: "the lack of to do so on each side leans in the other direction".

The whole middle of the video is a live bar-by-bar application from the RTH open, and it is
unusually good teaching: each new short-term extreme is a fresh test, the side that fails leans
the other way, and the read updates continuously. Three refinements emerge along the way. He
prefers the reference level to be a **higher-timeframe** low rather than a tiny intraday one — "I
prefer to wait for a higher time frame low to be broken". When *both* sides fail in sequence he
names the result: "you fail one side of the range you want to see the other side of the range
that's your broad information or your power of three… consolidation manipulation then you'd want
to get your distribution". And an aggressive move out immediately met by an aggressive move back
in is a BPR / box setup on the lower timeframe.

He also contrasts the two cases visually in a way a detector could use: "you can see how these
candles close below this structure versus here you can see one close immediately back into the
range".

### Rules stated

- Displacement = large aggressive candles closing at their own high/low, *over* structure.
- Buy side of the curve: lows taken without displacement are long entries.
- Sell side: highs taken without displacement are short entries.
- Prefer a higher-timeframe reference level over a tiny intraday one.
- Both sides failing ⇒ broadening formation / power of three; expect the untaken side next.
- Aggressive out then aggressive back in ⇒ BPR / box setup.

### Open questions

- "Not very aggressive" is the operative judgement on every chart, unquantified except by the
  candle-shape test above and the comparative procedure in `1oco9lesido`.
- "Higher time frame low" has no specific timeframe attached.
- "Side of the curve" is asserted from the chart rather than derived.
- No stop or target is given for these entries.

## What is Displacement? (`1oco9lesido`, https://youtu.be/1oco9lesido, 148s)

### What is actually taught

**The single most valuable 148 seconds in this unit, and possibly the most valuable answer in the
corpus to the question the library keeps flagging.** It is the only place anywhere that the
channel gives a *measurement procedure* for displacement rather than an adjective.

The frame: "when you break this short-term High you want to see an energetic move up". He then
shows a break that is *not* energetic, and rather than gesturing at it he measures it. "if you
just take these four candles when we broke this High here you have a range about like that" — and
separately, the displacement or **distance over the short-term high** is "about that large". He
then shows a second break of the same kind and repeats the identical measurement: "these four
candles that broke it right you have this energetic move up… the range is the difference between
this one and this one and if you look at the distance covered from there to there We compare
those you can see **in the same amount of time right we have a larger range and more distance
covered or broken through more price going over that short-term high**".

So the procedure is: fix a candle window (four, in his worked comparison), and compute two
quantities — the **range** of that window, and the **distance travelled beyond the broken
short-term high** — then compare against an instance he labels non-energetic. Displacement is the
case with the larger range *and* the greater distance in the same number of candles. It is a
*relative* test, not an absolute threshold, but it is a test, and it is implementable.

He closes with the consequence, which is the standard one but stated as the "most important
part": once displacement is confirmed, mark the displacement range and buy only at a discount of
it.

### Rules stated

- After a short-term high is broken, take a fixed candle window (four in his example).
- Compute the window's range and the distance covered beyond the broken high.
- Compare against a non-energetic break of the same kind; displacement has the larger range and
  greater distance in the same number of candles.
- Then look for the fair value gap setup, and buy only in the discount of the displacement range.

### Open questions

- Whether the window is fixed at four candles or is simply "the same amount of time" is not
  decidable from the transcript.
- The test is relative and needs a reference instance on the same chart; no absolute threshold
  (ATR multiple, body ratio, point value) is ever given.
- It says nothing about wick size directly; the closest the unit comes to that is the "closes in
  the high or the low" shape test in `5rbFskdmEmU`.

## Cross-video observations

1. **The corpus's two missing quantifications are partly answered here, and only here.** For
   "aggressive"/"displacement" there are now two procedures: the candle-shape test (large candles
   whose *closes are at their own high or low*, and over structure — `5rbFskdmEmU`) and the
   comparative test (fixed candle window; compare range and distance-beyond-the-level against a
   non-energetic instance — `1oco9lesido`). Neither is an absolute threshold, but both are
   implementable. For **wick size specifically, this unit yields nothing** — no ratio, no
   percentage, no procedure. The "closes in the high or the low" phrasing implies a small
   close-side wick and is the closest thing available.

2. **CISD does not exist yet.** "Change in the state of delivery" and "CISD" appear zero times in
   twenty videos. Everything the later material routes through CISD is routed here through
   "break structure" / "market structure shift". This dates the CISD vocabulary and means the
   unsettled question — *which* price of the opposing-candle series a CISD must close through —
   cannot be answered from this unit, because the object does not exist in it.

3. **"Kill zone" does not exist either, and no session in this unit carries a clock time.** The
   best that can be extracted is participation practice: New York a.m. is what he trades, the
   afternoon and the overnight are not — and even that is contradicted inside the unit by the
   PM-session trade in `wwMjc9RfpCg`. Recorded as `new-york-am-session-only`, `underspecified`.

4. **The SMT material contradicts itself inside one playlist.** `eHQ4nE-TQ2w` makes SMT directly
   tradeable with its own invalidation level and a mechanical asset-selection rule; `q1NmxUTm4n4`
   makes it confluence-only with discretionary asset selection. Both are preserved. The
   invalidation level from the first is genuinely new to the library and is the thing that makes
   SMT backtestable as a standalone signal.

5. **Timeframe selection is more explicit in the old material than the new.** Two decidable
   mappings appear here and nowhere else: entry timeframe selects the point-of-interest timeframes
   (`wDXTIDbIMwU`), and the 15-minute governs the 5-minute for trend reads (`FdRKBTz0Fps`). The
   later canon fixes ladders by example instead.

6. **Sub-minute charts are normal here and vanish later.** 15-second charts are used in four
   videos and a 5-second chart in one. The later material's lowest timeframe is the 1-minute.
   Along with the Strat, this is the clearest sign of a method being simplified over time.

7. **One entry skeleton recurs, same as in the updated unit, but with a different validator.**
   Liquidity swept → structure broken *with displacement* → retracement into a discount → fair
   value gap. The later canon replaces "structure broken with displacement" with "CISD", and adds
   a required prior engagement with a higher-timeframe level. The skeleton is otherwise identical.

8. **Attribution is already consistent this early.** AM Trades is credited by name for the
   standard-deviation projections. The 2022 mentorship model and ICT are referenced as the
   baseline he is departing from.
