## Market Structure Shift vs Change In State Of Delivery (`E89d1HArbgM`, 561s)

### What is actually taught

This is the most important video in the unit and the one the library needed. He defines
both concepts side by side in a PDF and then demonstrates the difference on charts,
including two cases where they disagree.

**Market structure shift** is a change in trend — "it is when we shift from bullish to
bearish or from bearish to bullish" — and mechanically it is "displacement over or below a
swing high or swing low". The example: a swing low forms, liquidity is swept, then
displacement and a close *below that swing low*.

**CISD** is explicitly the other thing. He says the change in the state of delivery is
**not** focused on the close below the swing high or swing low; it is "focused on the close
and displacement below the opening price of this up close" — the full sentence continues
"…or series of up close candles into an important level validating it as an order block."
So the CISD level is the **opening price of the opposing candle or series of opposing
candles that delivered price into the level**, and the close through it is simultaneously
what validates that block as an order block.

The whole back half of the video is the consequence: "the change in the state of delivery
happens prior to the market structure shift". Four examples make the point in both
directions. In one, CISD and MSS print on the same candle. In the second, CISD fires, price
runs straight to the old low with *no retracement*, and the MSS trader never gets an entry —
"it can provide an earlier entry to Market structure shifts". In the third, CISD fires,
MSS never happens, and price goes the other way: an earlier entry is "not always a good
thing" because "it may get you into a position that ends up to be a loser". The last
example on oil futures runs all three order-flow signals — inversion, CISD, MSS — and shows
inversion and CISD arriving days before the MSS.

Two secondary rules are worth keeping. On **inversions**, price closed through 50% of the
bullish FVG but not fully below it; he refuses to count that — "I prefer to wait for a full
closure below". On **entry refinement**, once the block is validated he takes the retest,
and when that gives only 1.1 R he refines: first the fair value gap inside it, then "find
the 0.5 or mean threshold of the order block". He closes by saying the choice between the
three signals is preference, not hierarchy: "I personally prefer to use order blocks".

### Rules stated

- MSS = displacement + close beyond a prior swing high/low. It is a trend-change signal.
- CISD = displacement + close beyond the **opening price** of the opposing candle / series
  that ran into an important level; that close validates the order block.
- CISD generally precedes MSS; sometimes they coincide; sometimes CISD fires and MSS never does.
- CISD's benefit is the earlier entry; its cost is entries the MSS filter would have vetoed.
- Inversion requires a **full** closure through the gap, not a 50% closure.
- MSS entry protocol: after the shift, wait for a retracement into a premium (short) / discount
  (long) PD array. If there is no retracement, there is no MSS trade.
- Entry refinement ladder when R is poor: order-block retest → FVG inside it → 0.5 / mean threshold.

### Open questions

- The PDF says "close **and displacement**" for CISD, but every chart narration collapses to
  "we close below the series of up close candles". Whether an FVG must exist in the CISD leg
  is unresolved even here.
- For a *series* of opposing candles, which candle's open is the level is never said in words
  (the charts use the series' outermost open).
- The "into an important level" clause is part of the definition but is not re-checked in the
  chart examples.

## Market Structure Shift (`_94CPMjWi9E`, 487s)

### What is actually taught

The standalone MSS lesson, and it front-loads displacement: "displacement is an aggressive
move with full-bodied candles", single or multiple, and "the easiest way to spot it is just
looking for fair value gaps". On a higher timeframe the same move is one candle.

MSS itself is then "what indicates a change in trend"; identification is displacement below
a previous low, or above a previous high for the bullish case. He adds two conditions the
CISD video does not: "I also prefer to have a stop rate prior to this", which also creates a
breaker formation and puts high-resistance liquidity behind the stop; and the one he calls
the most important thing he looks for — "if price engaged a higher time frame level prior to
shifting structure", without which "I do not personally search for a setup".

The three examples are drilled the same way: mark the HTF level (previous day high; previous
week's low; a daily FVG), wait for engagement, then require the sweep and the aggressive
close through the swing. He rejects one break explicitly because "we did not aggressively
move below this low", and prefers a sweep that does not close over the swept high. After the
shift he fibs the displacement range and buys the discount / sells the premium, looking for
an order block, breaker or OTE.

### Rules stated

- Displacement = aggressive full-bodied move; identify by the FVG it leaves.
- MSS = displacement below a previous low (bearish) / above a previous high (bullish).
- Preferred: a stop raid immediately before the break (gives a breaker + high-resistance
  liquidity for the stop).
- Required: price engaged a higher-timeframe level before the shift. No engagement, no setup.
- After the shift, look for a retracement into a PD array in the premium/discount of the
  displacement range.

### Open questions

- Sweep-first is "I also prefer" here but is definitional in `ynFA6E3qHj0` — preference vs
  requirement is not resolved anywhere in the unit.
- "Aggressive" and "full-bodied" carry no threshold.

## Market Structure Shift vs Liquidity Grab (`ynFA6E3qHj0`, 920s)

### What is actually taught

Definitional: "Market structure shift is when we get a sweep and then that large displacement
candle or candles closing below structure", and against it, "a liquidity grab simply put is a
failure to displace over structure". That is the cleanest statement of the pair in the corpus.

The genuinely valuable content is a three-way classification he applies every time price
returns to a marked old high or low, stated as an exhaustive list — "those are the three
things that I look for": price displaces out and retraces and continues; or displaces out,
displaces *back in*, and reverses; or fails to displace out and comes back in. He then walks
a full one-minute replay applying it bar by bar. In the third branch he wants price to return
to at least a premium (bearish) or discount (bullish) of the range before he trusts the
reversal.

The video ends honestly: on a one-minute chart there are so many structure breaks and
failures to displace that the classification is unusable alone — "it is important to have
some sort of directional bias". With a bearish bias he only looks for displacement down and
failures to displace up. A short coda previews using the same test on the daily for bias,
which becomes the next-day-model video.

### Rules stated

- Liquidity grab = reach beyond structure, no aggressive close outside, close back inside.
- MSS = sweep **then** displacement closing beyond structure.
- Three outcomes at an old high/low: (1) displace out → retrace → continue; (2) displace out →
  displace back in → reverse; (3) fail to displace → back into range → other side.
- Branch 3 wants a return to premium/discount of the range before the reversal is trusted.
- On low timeframes the read requires an external directional bias to be usable.

### Open questions

- Branch 1 vs branch 2 turns entirely on whether the move back in is "aggressive". Unquantified.
- No limit on how many candles may elapse before "displacement back in" stops counting.

## Important Time Levels For Trading (`EYzP7c24AwM`, 918s)

### What is actually taught

The primary source for the corpus's biggest blocking term. Six clock levels, each with a
stated reason: **18:00** is the new daily candle open; **midnight**; **08:30** — "8:30 is when
the news embargo lifts or there's high impact news"; **09:30** — "9:30 is when the New York
Stock Exchange opens"; **10:00** — "10:00 is when we have a new 4-Hour candle and sometimes
news"; and **14:00**, another 4-hour candle.

Timezone is settled outright: "all times are shown in Eastern Standard time". **This confirms
the EST / New York clock.** The 4-hour grid is also confirmed but only partially: he says
"10:00 and 2:00 will be a 4-Hour candle" and later narrates a **06:00** 4-hour candle in a
worked example, which together imply 18 / 22 / 02 / 06 / 10 / 14 New York. He never lists the
six boundaries.

**One recent library reading is not supported by this source and should be treated as
unconfirmed:** nothing in this video says 10:00 opens a 4H *and* 1H *and* 30m *and* 15m
together. Only the 4-hour is named.

**And one important refinement that the library did not have:** "these time openings are
going to be slightly different between asset classes", because on forex "we don't really have
the 930 open with Forex" and the 4-hour candles change. So the 18/22/02/06/10/14 grid is an
index-futures grid, not a universal one.

The usage split is three-way and explicit. 18:00 and midnight are used with the **daily**
candle's open-high-low-close; 10:00 and 14:00 with the **4-hour** candle's. Midnight and 08:30
are additionally support/resistance and premium/discount markers — bearish and trading above
both is "a deep premium". And midnight, 08:30 and 09:30 are where he hunts a Judas swing: "I
also look for a Judas swing or an order block formation at midnight", and "if I'm bullish I
want to see opposing runs at these times"; the run "makes an opposing run closes over that's
now an order block", after which "I don't expect this low to be taken as we continue higher".

Six chart examples chain all of it together: bias from the daily → premium/discount from
midnight and 08:30 → Judas swing at 08:30 → distribution at 09:30 → the 4-hour open-low-high-close
framed off 10:00 → retracement at 14:00.

### Rules stated

- Mark 18:00, 00:00, 08:30, 09:30, 10:00, 14:00 New York (EST).
- 18:00 + midnight → daily OHLC framing. 10:00 + 14:00 → 4-hour OHLC / power of three.
- Midnight + 08:30 → support/resistance and premium/discount. Above both while bearish = deep premium.
- Midnight, 08:30, 09:30 → Judas swing / opposing run forming an order block.
- Once an opposing run is closed over, its extreme should not be revisited.
- A retracement inside a directional 4-hour sequence is expected at the *next* 4-hour open.
- Asset class changes the grid; forex has no 09:30 and different 4-hour boundaries.

### Open questions

- "Eastern **Standard** Time" is said literally; whether he re-anchors across DST is never addressed.
- The forex 4-hour grid is flagged as different but never given.
- "At" a time level is not bounded — how many minutes after 08:30 an opposing run still counts.

## ICT Silver Bullet Strategy — No Daily Bias | With Backtest! (`o0v4KQxZbpU`, 1748s)

### What is actually taught

A bias-free session model on NQ plus the only quantified backtest in the unit.

The model: at 10:00 New York, mark the high and low of the **09:00 hourly candle**. Wait for
one side to be taken; target the other side. "these entries have to fall within 10 to 11:
a.m. window" — outside it he does not look, and an order unfilled at 11:00 is cancelled. The
framework is imported from his intraday-bias video: "the previous hours high and low or the
9:00 a.m. hourly candle". Entry after the sweep is any of his usual models — breaker, order
block, fair value gap — chosen on the fly.

The backtest is run live in FX Replay, day by day, on CFD NAS100 with a $100,000 balance and
1% risk. Management is fixed: "once this hits 3r I will move my stop to break even". He
narrates roughly a third of sessions as no-trade (no sweep, or no entry before 11:00), and
several more are declined on discretion — "not a fan of that price action or these sweeps in
here", "I prefer the entry on the cell side of the curve not when price is coming back".
At least three stop-outs are taken on camera.

**The numbers, exactly as stated:** "our average trade was an hour and 29 minutes took 15
trades" over 45 days; "July we ended up with 23.5% and August so far with 16.93%" for a total
of 44.4%; "average RR of about five". And his own caveat, unprompted: "I only tested this over
45 days and 15 trades", and a deeper backtest would be needed.

### Conditions the numbers were measured under (recorded verbatim in the concept file)

Instrument CFD NAS100 (NASDAQ / NQ). Balance $100,000. Risk 1% per trade. Window 10:00–11:00
AM only. Period all of July plus roughly the first half of August (year never stated), which
he calls 45 days. Stop to break-even at 3R. Entry selection **discretionary** within the
model. Win rate is never reported, nor loss count, nor drawdown.

### Open questions

- The model as taught is not mechanical: which entry model to use, and whether a given sweep
  is convincing, are live judgements that materially changed the trade count.
- 45 *calendar* days, not trading days; the trade count implies far fewer sessions traded.
- No win rate, no max drawdown, no year.

## AMD & Standard Deviations (`Pcbyc9TdNsU`, 659s)

### What is actually taught

Definitions first: "accumulation is essentially some sort of consolidation manipulation is a
fake breakout" (or the false move), and "distribution is the real move in the opposite
direction of the manipulation".

The projection arithmetic, captured mechanically: focus on the **manipulation**, and inside
it, "we want to see the leg that made the low before the high" — the terminal swing of the
manipulation, which he notes is also the breaker formation. Then "from here we'll take the
high to the low and project it out". Targets: "I want to look for is the -2 to 2.5 standard
deviations", then the fourth standard deviation. In a third example a **-1** level lines up
with the start of a fair value gap and is used as the target.

The gate between the two target bands is stated: after the -2/-2.5 reaction he requires price
to respect the up-close candles (an order block) before he expects the fourth deviation.

Three examples: (1) equal highs → consolidation → sweep → "we want to see display placement
back into the range and breaking structure or changing the state of delivery"; entry at the
CISD/OB/FVG, first target -2, then -4 after the OB holds. (2) The **box setup paired with
projections** — mark the consolidation by the bodies, entry at the highest bodies extended
over, stop on the manipulation high, target the second deviation. (3) **Session AMD** —
"I like to see Asia accumulate London manipulate and then New York distribute"; project the
London manipulation leg, and the -2/-2.5 lands on a failure swing which becomes the
"low hanging fruit" target.

### Rules stated

- AMD: accumulation = consolidation; manipulation = fake breakout; distribution = the real move.
- To confirm manipulation, require aggressive displacement back into the range plus MSS or CISD.
  Without it, expect continuation instead.
- Project from the manipulation high to the low that made it (mirrored for the bullish case).
- First target -2 to -2.5 (a retracement/reversal area); second target the 4th deviation, taken
  only if the order block at the -2/-2.5 reaction holds.
- -1 is used when it coincides with an independent PD array.
- Session form: Asia accumulate, London manipulate, New York distribute.

### Open questions

- Which endpoint the projection is anchored *from* is shown on charts but never stated in words.
- Sign convention flips between examples (-2/-4 downside vs 4/4.5 upside in `hKCMqh0ZsY0`).
- No rule orders a deviation target against a competing structural target.

## A+ ICT Entry Checklist (`0xygpCMwxbQ`, 659s)

### What is actually taught

A four-item ordered gate applied **after** a daily bias already exists. "the first thing I
focus on is a stop raid" — a low was put in and then run. "the next thing I look for is a
market structure shift or displacement" — a previous high broken and aggressively moved over.
Third, and the one place he grants slack, "I generally like to see price move back into a
discount of this price range". Fourth, a PD array in that area: he prefers a fair value gap or
an order block, and will also use an OTE or a breaker block. Each could be an entry model
alone; he blends one, two or three.

The selection logic among candidates is explicitly a trade-off, not a rule: "as I move my
entry closer to my stop loss my risk to reward increases", against the falling odds of a fill,
so he balances R, fill probability, and what catches his eye.

One hard gate does emerge, in the fourth example: he refuses a displacement move because there
was no prior sweep, and states "without a lower time frame liquidity rate prior to a move I am
not interested in it". The same example is then admitted as a *box setup* entry with no
structure shift at all — which he flags as a deliberate exception.

The final example runs daily → hourly (stop raid + displacement, fib to equilibrium) → 15m →
5m, and rejects an otherwise-valid fair value gap purely because it sat in a premium of the range.

### Rules stated

1. Stop raid. 2. MSS / displacement. 3. Return to a discount (premium for shorts); OTE narrows it.
4. A PD array there — FVG, OB, OTE or breaker; blended preferred.
- No lower-timeframe liquidity raid before the move → no interest, regardless of displacement.
- Reject a PD array on the wrong side of equilibrium.
- Entry choice = risk-to-reward vs fill probability.

### Open questions

- The "flexibility" on the discount requirement is unbounded; one example skips step 2 entirely.
- Choosing among the four PD arrays is "what catches my eye the most".

## Box Setup — The Easiest Entry Model? (`HFfChGFHD44`, 511s)

### What is actually taught

Resting liquidity (an old high/low or a consolidation range) is taken by an aggressive move
**out** of the range, then met by an aggressive move **back into** it. That excursion is the
"deviation from the range", and the trade is its retest.

The load-bearing condition is stated as such: "this move out and back into the range needs to
be aggressive". And the *reason* is given, which is unusual for this channel — "this is what
forms the wick on a higher time frame". He also says outright that the pattern is power of
three: accumulation, manipulation, distribution.

Mechanics: "I generally Mark out the bodies for this setup", and the entry is "when we retest
this deviation from the range with my stop on the current high", targeting the other side of
the range. Two documented deviations from that default: when there is no identifiable range,
wait for displacement over highs, then look left at which highs were run and mark the highest
body; and when the body level would sit too far from the extreme, use the wick instead.
For bias on the 4-hour he uses bodies, but "out to the Daily this is when I generally will
focus on just the Wicks", then drops to the 15-minute for setups.

### Rules stated

- Range → aggressive move out → aggressive move back in → retest of the deviation = entry.
- Mark bodies by default; wicks on the daily, and wicks when the body level is too far.
- Stop on the current high/low (the manipulation extreme); target the other side of the range.
- No aggressive return = no setup.
- Fallback range construction: displacement over highs, then look left at the highs run.

### Open questions

- "Aggressive" is unquantified in both directions and is the only decision the model turns on.
- Whether the return leg must *close* inside the range or merely trade inside is not stated.

## Breaker Block & FVG Entry Model (`rXMVPUZPw4A`, 468s)

### What is actually taught

The unicorn: "The unicorn is a breaker block paired with a fair value gap." He defines both
components first. FVG is the three-candle pattern where "the first candlestick's low will not
overlap with the third candlesticks high" (mirrored for bullish).

The **breaker** definition is structural and is the crispest in the corpus: swing high, swing
low, sweep of the swing high making a higher high, then displacement below the swing low
making a lower low — "it is a high low higher high lower low". The traded candle is then
narrowed precisely: "I'm focused on the lowest down close candle prior to the move that went"
and made the higher high / raided stops. Bullish mirrors.

The unicorn is the overlap of that breaker with a fair value gap left by the displacement leg.
Three examples on NASDAQ (2m, 1m) and one that never fills — "that is an example of being left
behind" — which is a useful negative. Stop placement is offered two ways on the same trade:
on the swept low (2.3R) or just beyond the breaker (5R). He notes wicks may dig into the
breaker as long as the bodies do not close beyond its mean threshold, and cross-references
the standard-deviation projection as extra confluence. He credits the model to Tyler and Ash
and their 40-page PDF: "I would suggest checking out Tyler and Ash on Twitter".

### Rules stated

- Breaker = high, low, higher high (sweep), lower low (displacement). Bullish mirrors.
- The block is the lowest down-close candle before the leg that made the higher high.
- Unicorn = that breaker **plus an overlapping** fair value gap; the overlap is the entry.
- Bodies must respect the mean threshold; wicks may not.
- Stop on the swept extreme or just beyond the breaker — a risk-to-reward choice.
- Draw on liquidity (equal / smooth highs or lows) is marked before the setup.

### Open questions

- Whether the higher high must *close* above the swing high or only wick above (diagrams show a wick).
- "Overlapping" is not quantified.
- He teaches and credits the model; whether it is part of his own routine is not stated.

## Broadening Formations — ICT Seek & Destroy (`Tjk9bXERZy0`, 777s)

### What is actually taught

"a broadening formation is a pattern in which price is constantly expanding": from a range,
price takes one side, fails to break out, trades back through and takes the other side, and
the range ends larger than it started. The directional read is the whole point — "if it's
failing this side where is it likely to go back through" the range again.

Two drawing methods. Right-to-left: take a high and a low and connect the highs/lows each of
them took out. Or, the one he says works better for most people, mark buyside and sellside
liquidity, extend the line when each side is taken, and keep extending as new extremes print.

The higher-timeframe identity is the useful mechanical fact: "a broadening information on a
higher time frame will be an outside bar", since that candle took both the range high and the
range low. He demonstrates this on the daily with previous day high and low both taken.

Four pairings are given: SMT at the edges of the range (because "in consolidations I'm only
interested at the external part of the range"); power of three, with the manipulation taking
out the whole consolidation; the box setup, using the initial range as a point of interest;
and premium/discount, either fixed on the initial range or updated as the range expands.
Targets are either the opposing liquidity or the broadening line itself.

The last section is the **Seek & Destroy** link: "if London session took Asia high and low
then I'm anticipating New York" to use London's high and low as points of interest and/or a
broadening formation. Three days are worked through on a 15-minute chart with an indicator
he calls `_boxes` (settings adjusted, not disclosed). And the regime condition: "I mostly
anticipate this daily profile when there is a lack of news events" on the calendar, or on a
day waiting for tomorrow's release.

### Rules stated

- Failed breakout of one side → expect rotation to the other side.
- Extend the liquidity lines each time a new extreme is taken.
- HTF signature = outside bar (both previous extremes taken).
- Only the external part of a consolidation is of interest.
- Session nesting: London takes both Asia extremes without displacing → New York uses London's extremes.
- Anticipate this profile on empty-calendar days.

### Open questions

- Broadening formations nest at every scale; no rule selects the operative one.
- Bodies vs wicks for the initial range is explicitly optional.
- No rule for how to *trade* the profile beyond using session extremes as points of interest.

## How To Use The Economic Calendar For Trading (`hKCMqh0ZsY0`, 785s)

### What is actually taught

The most fully mechanical video in the unit, and one of the few places the channel gives a
hard, non-discretionary filter.

Filtering: forexfactory.com → calendar → filter → select the currency of the instrument
(USD for NQ/ES; both AUD and CAD for AUDCAD) → "I want to focus just on the red folder or high
impact news".

Then he **sub-tiers the red folder** into high / medium / low impact and gives each a rule,
crediting AM Trades: "with high impact news I'm going to avoid the day prior avoid the
release" and then watch after; "with medium impact I don't need to avoid the day prior" but
avoid the session prior, avoid the release, watch after; and "low impact I have no
restrictions". FOMC, NFP and CPI are treated as high; PPI as medium.

On top sits his own standing rule: "I avoid trading prior to the first high impact news event
of the week" "as well as skipping Mondays". And the NFP protocol, also credited to AM Trades:
"my interest lies mainly on Friday post news release", and "I will also watch Tuesday and
Wednesday but avoid Thursday".

The output is computed at the start of the week: in the first example (FOMC Wednesday, NFP
Friday) only two days survive. Three worked examples then show the filter feeding the weekly
profile — a midweek reversal confirmed by a CISD, a consolidation reversal with a Friday
continuation, and an AUDCAD post-CPI trade. All three use standard-deviation projections
(4 / 4.5 as the far target) and introduce the **propulsion block**: down-close candles into an
existing order block, of which "ideally I wouldn't like to see price trade or close below the
mean threshold here so half of the bodies".

### Rules stated

- Filter to the instrument's currency and the red folder only.
- High impact: avoid day prior, avoid release, watch after.
- Medium impact: day prior allowed, avoid session prior, avoid release, watch after.
- Low impact: no restriction.
- Skip Monday. Do not trade before the week's first high-impact event.
- NFP week: Friday post-release is the focus; watch Tue/Wed; avoid Thursday.
- Propulsion block: do not want price trading or closing below its mean threshold.

### Open questions

- The high/medium/low sub-tiering of red-folder events is a slide list, not a derivable rule.
- "Avoid the session prior" assumes a session grid this video does not define.
- Whether the Monday skip yields to a Monday high-impact release is not addressed.

## ICT Advanced Market Structure Simplified (`umyCqr_0NsI`, 843s)

### What is actually taught

A three-tier swing labelling scheme, credited up front — "shout out to the mxm trader for his
teachings on Advanced Market structure".

Base tier: "a short-term low is when we form a swing low" (a low with a higher low each side)
and "a short-term high is a high with a lower high on each side". Middle tier: an
intermediate-term low is "a short-term low with a higher short-term low on the right and left"
of it. There is a second kind — the **rebalanced** intermediate-term point — created "when
price rebalance is a fair value Gap" and trades away. Top tier: a long-term low is "the low
that is formed off the reaction of the higher time frame level", validated once a short-term
low prints on its right side.

## Internal & External Liquidity Model (`mwmWNCTEYtY`, 978s)

### What is actually taught

Definitions: "internal liquidity is just a fair value Gap"; "external liquidity is simply
highs and lows" — swing highs and lows, i.e. buyside and sellside. The rotation: price sweeps
external liquidity, fails to displace through it, reaches back for internal liquidity, respects
the gap, and returns to external — "price moves from external to internal and back to external".

The model built on it is a clean three-timeframe stack. Step 1: a higher-timeframe level.
Step 2: one timeframe down, read the internal/external rotation to get the draw. Step 3: on
the execution timeframe, "make sure I'm in a Kill Zone I have a stop raid", then "a market
structure shift a fair value Gap and then I will Target to R". The 2R is a *fixed* target, not
a floor — twice he notes runners would have paid more and declines them ("I'm just interested
in catching two r with this model").

Practical additions: when the area around the entry is messy, step **up** a timeframe to place
the stop above a clean inversion or a balanced area rather than in the noise; when aggressive
displacement is met with displacement back the other way, expect continuation instead
("expansion into expansion"); and when the retracement is against the HTF bias he waits for it
to complete and trades the expansion out of it rather than trading the retracement.

He names his own preferred ladder: "I personally prefer a daily hour and 5 minute setups" as
it aligns with the formation of the daily candle. Two higher-timeframe demonstrations
(monthly → daily → hourly, and weekly → 4H → 15m) are included for people who cannot watch intraday.

### Rules stated

- Internal liquidity = fair value gap. External = swing highs/lows.
- Ext → int when the sweep fails to displace; int → ext once the gap is respected and price closes away.
- A mitigated gap is no longer the internal draw.
- Execution requires all four: kill zone, stop raid, market structure shift, fair value gap.
- Fixed 2R target.
- Messy stop area → move the stop reference up a timeframe.
- Expansion met with expansion → continuation, not reversal.
- Preferred ladder daily / 1H / 5m; the whole ladder can be shifted up.

### Open questions

- "Kill zone" is required but never defined in this video (London and New York are used).
- Whether "market structure shift" here means the swing-break test or the CISD test is not disambiguated.
- No rule selects among several unmitigated gaps inside one range.
