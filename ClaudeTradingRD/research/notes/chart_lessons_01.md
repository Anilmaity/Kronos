# chart_lessons_01 — Chart Lessons (batch 1 of 1)

Unit: `chart_lessons_01` · playlist "Chart Lessons" · 4 videos · **4 transcripts
present** (3 from YouTube captions; #1 recovered by local speech recognition).

**Episode #1 (`Nlw-PZhoViQ`) is an ASR transcript, not a caption transcript.** That
video publishes no caption track at all — not auto-generated, not manual — so the
original crawl came back empty and the unit was first studied from #2–#4 only. It
has since been recovered locally with `whisper-small` (`transcript_source:
whisper-small` in the file header). Speech recognition mishears the domain jargon
that captions get right: "CISD", "candle 2", "2R", "FVG" and numeric levels are all
garbled in places. Everything below is therefore verbatim **with respect to that
file**, which is not the same as verbatim with respect to what was said. Where the
wording is clearly mangled but the meaning is recoverable from the surrounding
narration, the reading is recorded in the concept file's `ambiguities` and flagged
as ASR-derived rather than being silently corrected. Every concept citing this video
carries an explicit ASR caveat in `ambiguities`.

These are applied post-trade walkthroughs, not lectures. The value in them is the
**order of operations**: what gets marked on the chart first, what is required
before dropping a timeframe, what is treated as confirmation, and — importantly —
what is explicitly dismissed as irrelevant. The same skeleton runs through all
four: classify the higher-timeframe phase → find a candle 2 closure → confirm with
a change in the state of delivery on the timeframe below → locate a protected swing
→ refine the entry downward until risk-to-reward is acceptable → manage against
protected swings.

## TTrades Top-Down Chart Lessons #1
(video_id: `Nlw-PZhoViQ`, https://youtu.be/Nlw-PZhoViQ, 965s — **ASR-recovered**)

### What is actually taught (detailed)

The opening episode of the series, and structurally the odd one out: it is not a
trade review but a **replay**. He steps EURUSD forward one candle at a time on the
daily and narrates the classification decision at each close, then does the same
thing on the hourly, the 30-minute, the 15-minute and the 3-minute. There is no
single trade with an entry, a stop and a result; there are half a dozen candidate
entries, several of which he declines. The framing statement is only that he intends
to blend the channel's concepts into the current week's price action.

The daily read is run entirely as a decision tree. After an expansion lower, he says
the next phase must be one of exactly three things — a retracement that continues
lower, a consolidation, or expansion back higher — and he tests each candle against
that list as it closes. When neither retracement nor consolidation fits, he concludes
it is expansion the other way, and states the rule that follows: expansion met with
expansion is a reversal. That is the same mechanic Chart Lesson #2 calls a V-shaped
reversal, stated here as a phase identity rather than a shape.

The most useful passage in the unit is the **candle-shape tie-break**. A daily candle
closes back inside its range and he says outright that the daily chart does not
contain enough information to decide anything. So he drops to the hourly and asks one
question about the order of construction: did price open, trade lower, then higher,
then close back in — or open, trade higher, then lower, then close back in. Because
it opened and made its high first, he reads it as more of a bearish candle *despite
the bullish close*. He then says what the ideal version would have been (a much weaker
close, into the lows), draws it, and explains what that would have bought him: two
opposing candles, a protected high, and a shallow retracement to anticipate. Since the
actual close is bullish and late in the day, the consequence is not that the bias dies
but that the confirmation requirement rises — he will need more on the lower
timeframes before getting short. Quality, in this video, is a dial on how much
confirmation is demanded, not a gate.

Protected swings are worked negatively for most of the video, which is what makes it
valuable. He zooms in on a candidate level, asks whether price actually closed below
it, answers no, and concludes the level is *not* a protected swing. Going into the
next day he finds there are none at all, and instead of abandoning the bias he
enumerates the only two ways one can appear: price retraces up into the higher
timeframe fair value gap and trades back down through it, which would create two sets
of opposing candles and protect two highs at once; or price fails to do that, runs the
highs out first, and forms a new protected high up there. He then watches which
branch arrives. The second one does.

Failure swings get a reading that does not appear in #2 or #4. There, they are
dismissed as irrelevant structure. Here they are a positive expectation: with failure
swings on the highs there is no protected swing to short against, so he refuses to get
onside — and then says that when failure swings are present, that is when he wants to
see those levels revisited. Price duly comes back and takes the equal highs out.

Two further generalisations are stated rather than merely demonstrated. First, the
**failed-closure tendency**: when price cannot close below the level it needs, the high
usually gets taken out. He says it, applies it, and then shows an instance where it
does not happen — the closure eventually arrives without the high being taken. Second,
the **consolidation test**: he frames a sequence as expansion → retracement → expansion,
identifies the closure that would confirm the second expansion, and when that closure
repeatedly fails to print, he reclassifies — "we are now just consolidating" — and
switches to watching only the range high and low. That is the single most decision-
useful thing in the unit, because it is the one mechanical answer to the
consolidation-versus-retracement question that #2 and #3 decide by stated distrust.

The entry work is a menu rather than a trade. Because the hourly is already forming
its upper wick, he does not expect a swing point there, so he drops a timeframe — a
second, R-independent reason for refinement that #2 does not give. On the 30-minute he
takes a protected swing, puts the stop above the high, and then moves the stop down to
50% of the opposing candle to reach his multiple, justifying it directionally: he does
not expect price to trade back above that half. He then re-runs the identical logic on
the 15-minute (expansion → retracement → protected swing on the closure below) and on
the 3-minute inside a fractal 30-minute/3-minute pairing, showing that the same
structure is available at three scales and that which one you take is a choice about
stop size, not about validity. On the 3-minute he takes an entry he *cannot* get his
multiple on against the nearest low, and says that is fine because he is targeting the
higher-timeframe objectives — which is in tension with #2's refusal to extend a target.

Two stand-aside decisions close the video out. A consolidation forms and he states the
general form of the rule: inside a consolidation you want the high taken out first,
then price runs the lows. But that same high is a swing he does not want taken if the
day is to continue lower — so the two conditions point at one price, and he says he
will **avoid price** there because he has conflicting ideas. The conflict resolves
itself: price breaks down out of the range without touching the level. He accepts the
breakout as legitimate, then declines to trade it, waiting instead for a continuation
entry after it. The trend is finally called over when a newly formed protected high is
taken and price stops respecting the opposing candles — confirmed one timeframe up,
where the 30-minute prints a bullish reversal candle.

### Concepts introduced (speaker's own terminology)

- Phases of price as a three-way decision tree tested at every close.
- Expansion met with expansion = a reversal = a wick on the higher timeframe.
- Reading the daily candle by the order of its extremes (open-high-low-close).
- A weak close "into the lows" as the ideal, and a strong close against the bias as a
  reason to demand more lower-timeframe confirmation.
- Protected swing tested negatively ("this level does not form a protected swing").
- The two ways a protected swing can form when none exists.
- Failure swings as a reason the levels will be revisited.
- Failure to close below the level → the high usually gets taken out.
- "We are now just consolidating" as the outcome of a failed continuation closure.
- 0.5 of the change in the state of delivery, and a "conflict" when the close is over it.
- 0.5 of the opposing candle used as a stop level, not only as a retest filter.
- The fractal 30-minute and 3-minute setup.
- "Avoid price" because of conflicting ideas.
- Waiting for a continuation entry rather than trading a consolidation breakout.

### Rules / conditions stated (each decidable if possible)

1. After expansion, the next phase is exactly one of: retracement, consolidation, or
   expansion the other way. Test each candle against the list. (Decidable in form; the
   primitive judgements are still by eye.)
2. Expansion met with expansion is a reversal, and forms a wick one timeframe up.
   (Decidable given the leg labels.)
3. When the daily close is inconclusive, drop to the hourly and read the day by the
   order of its extremes: high first = bearish, low first = bullish. (Decidable.)
4. A bearish day ideally closes into its lows; a close that is not weak enough does not
   cancel the bias but raises the lower-timeframe confirmation requirement.
   ("More confirmation" is never specified — see ambiguities.)
5. A level is only a protected swing if price actually closed through it. Zoom in and
   check. (Decidable.)
6. With no protected swing, exactly two paths create one: retrace into the HTF fair
   value gap and close back through (protecting the highs in place), or run the highs
   out first and form a new protected high. (Decidable; no probability attached.)
7. Failure swings on a side mean no protected swing on that side — do not get onside;
   expect those levels to be revisited instead. (Decidable.)
8. Failure to close below the level → the high usually gets taken out. (Decidable, and
   stated as a tendency, not a certainty — he shows it failing.)
9. Frame the sequence as expansion → retracement → expansion and mark the closure that
   confirms the second expansion. If that closure will not print, it is a consolidation:
   stop waiting for the continuation and mark only the range high and low. (Decidable.)
10. In a consolidation the manipulation comes first — for a bearish continuation, the
    range high should be taken, then price runs the lows. (Decidable.)
11. If a required manipulation and a swing that must hold are the same level, the ideas
    conflict — avoid price until it resolves. (Decidable.)
12. Do not trade inside a consolidation and do not trade its breakout; wait for the
    continuation entry after it. (Decidable.)
13. If the current timeframe will not produce a swing point (the HTF wick is already
    forming), drop one timeframe. (Decidable.)
14. To reach the required multiple, move the stop to 0.5 of the opposing candle rather
    than moving the target — justified by not expecting price back above that half.
15. The trend is over once a new protected swing is taken and the opposing candles stop
    being respected; confirm one timeframe up. (Decidable.)

### Examples walked through

- **EURUSD, daily → hourly → 30m → 15m → 3m.** A week replayed candle by candle. Daily
  expansion lower, consolidation with failure swings on the highs, a sweep of the low
  and a bullish closure, a bullish daily close that is nonetheless read as bearish from
  its open-high-low-close shape, a day with no protected swings that resolves by running
  the highs, then a confirmed protected high and a continuation lower toward two
  previous-day lows. Entries are then demonstrated on the 30m (stop moved to 0.5 of the
  opposing candle), the 15m (stop above the highs), and the 3m (fractal 30m/3m), plus a
  consolidation sweep entry and a post-breakout continuation entry. No result is reported
  for any of them — this is a teaching replay, not a trade review.

### Open questions / ambiguities

- **ASR quality.** Good enough for the structural argument, not good enough for
  numbers or acronyms. "Change in the state of delivery" survives (it is spoken in
  full), but "2R" is rendered variously as "to R", "two R" and "R"; "fair value gap"
  appears once as "fair value get"; "wait for" appears as "wafer"; "look to short"
  appears as "look too short"; "retraced" appears as "wretched". No quote used
  anywhere in this unit depends on a garbled term.
- The three things this unit was asked to look for are **all absent**. There is no
  wick-vs-body threshold for classifying an expansion candle — "expansion" is asserted
  by eye throughout and no ratio is given. There are **no session or kill-zone hours
  at all**: the words session, London, New York, kill zone and open (in the timing
  sense) do not appear, and the entire replay is run on candle closures with no clock.
  And the CISD is never resolved to a specific price in an opposing-candle series — he
  points at levels on screen ("this level here to form that change in the state of
  delivery") without saying which price of which candle they are, and in one passage
  distinguishes the level that forms the opposing candle from the level that forms the
  CISD without explaining the difference.
- "More confirmation on the lower time frames" is the operative consequence of a
  degraded closure and is never specified — no list, no timeframe, no count.
- The failure-swing reading here (expect the levels to be revisited) sits alongside the
  dismissal of failure swings in #2 as basically irrelevant. Both are recorded; neither
  video acknowledges the other.
- Taking an entry that cannot reach the multiple against the nearest low, on the
  grounds that the higher-timeframe objective is the real target, is in direct tension
  with #2's rule never to extend the target to manufacture the ratio.
- Whether 0.5 of the opposing candle is measured across the bodies or the wicks is not
  stated, which matters because it is used here as a stop level.

## TTrades Top-Down Chart Lessons #2
(video_id: `UBTl7za9obc`, https://youtu.be/UBTl7za9obc, 742s)

### What is actually taught (detailed)

A full top-down review of a long trade on ES (S&P 500 futures). He begins on the
daily chart and reads it by phase, not by pattern: an aggressive move lower
followed by an aggressive move higher, evidenced by fair value gaps generated on
both sides of the market. He names that combination a V-shaped reversal, then
demonstrates the fractal consequence — he removes the drawings, moves up to the
weekly, and shows that a down-then-up sequence on the daily *is* a reversal candle
on the weekly. That weekly reversal candle is the bias: he expects the coming week
to trade higher and take out the previous high.

Returning to the daily, he classifies each day. Monday opens as a consolidation,
which he says makes sense following a large Friday expansion, and closes near its
low. Tuesday sweeps Monday's consolidation low and reverses — his candle 2. He
states plainly that the reversal itself is the harder trade and the continuation is
the easier one, so the working idea becomes: Tuesday reversed, therefore look for
Wednesday to expand higher, with the sequence expansion → retracement into a fair
value gap → new expansion.

The confirmation step is mechanical. He drops to the hourly, locates the reversal
point (the low), identifies the series of down-close candles that produced that
low, and requires a closure through that level. With that closure in hand he
anticipates the next candle trading higher, and he marks 0.5 of the previous candle
as the level he wants price to trade higher from. He also wants price to respect
the *upper half* of the daily candle, because a shallow wick is what supports
expansion.

Into the New York session he shows what he ignores: a small reversal off the
previous day high is dismissed because it only leaves failure swings, which he
calls basically irrelevant. He then refuses to call the subsequent pullback a
retracement, saying he does not trust it and that it is just a consolidation, and
applies the consolidation rule instead: mark the range high and low, and wait for
the range low to be swept into a point of interest, which here is a fair value gap
sitting in the upper half of the previous daily candle.

Asset selection is a short aside: he ranks YM, ES and NQ by strength, notes that
when YM is very strong NQ is the weakest, and therefore prefers ES longs over NQ
longs even though NQ is his usual instrument.

The entry work is the most procedurally dense part. On the 5-minute he finds a
bearish reversal at the 9:00 open going to maximum expansion, an aggressive move
down met with an aggressive move up (the higher-timeframe wick), and a change in
the state of delivery. He then overlaps 0.5 of the wick with 0.5 of the change in
the state of delivery — the T-spot — to define the area price must hold, and finds
a fair value gap inside it as the point of interest. He explains why he does not
just enter immediately: he needs to be able to exit or partial at or before the
short-term targets, and an entry that high does not leave enough room. He then
refines downward — 5-minute continuation, no acceptable risk-to-reward; 3-minute,
still not; and rarely, as here, down to the 1-minute — repeating the same wick /
closure / protected-low logic at each scale until 2R to the intraday highs is
achievable. He is explicit that he will not extend the target to manufacture the
ratio. Entry came on the break of the high with the stop on the 1-minute low,
targeting just over 4R; he partialled at 2R and trailed the remainder, which
eventually stopped out at the same level his take-profit had filled.

### Concepts introduced (speaker's own terminology)

- V-shaped reversal; aggressive move lower met with aggressive move higher.
- Fair value gaps on both sides of the market as evidence of the two aggressive legs.
- Higher-timeframe wick formed by lower-timeframe expansion down then expansion up.
- Phases of price: expansion, retracement, consolidation, reversal.
- Candle 2 (the reversal day) and the continuation that follows it.
- Change in the state of delivery (CISD).
- The T-spot — overlap of 0.5 of the wick and 0.5 of the CISD.
- Point of interest (a fair value gap or order block inside the T-spot).
- Protected low / protected swing.
- Failure swings (dismissed as irrelevant structure).
- Fractalizing the higher-timeframe read on the lower timeframe.
- Continuation entries sought repeatedly until risk-to-reward makes sense.

### Rules / conditions stated (each decidable if possible)

1. Aggressive move down immediately followed by aggressive move up, with fair value
   gaps on both sides, is a reversal; on the timeframe above it is a single reversal
   candle. (Decidable except that "aggressive" is only evidenced by the FVGs.)
2. Prefer the continuation after the reversal over the reversal itself.
3. Confirm a reversal by finding the series of down-close candles that made the low
   and requiring a closure through that level. (Decidable.)
4. Mark 0.5 of the previous candle; want price to trade higher from there.
5. Want price to respect the upper half of the daily candle — a small shallow wick
   supports expansion. (Decidable.)
6. If the pullback is a consolidation rather than a retracement, mark the range high
   and low and wait for the range low to be swept into a point of interest.
   (The consolidation-vs-retracement call is judgemental — see ambiguities.)
7. Structure that only leaves failure swings is irrelevant and does not change bias.
8. Do not take the entry at the top of the move; wait for the retrace so that the
   short-term targets are reachable at acceptable risk-to-reward.
9. Refine downward (5m → 3m → 1m) seeking continuations until at least ~2R is
   achievable; never extend the target to force the ratio. (Decidable.)
10. Default behaviour is to let the higher-timeframe candle close and trade the
    10:00 continuation; trading the reversal itself requires extra context.
11. After expansion → consolidation → break, want a retest and go; consolidation →
    break → push lower is read as a reversal and is the cue to be out.

### Examples walked through

- **ES (S&P 500 futures), daily → weekly → hourly → 5m → 3m → 1m.** Weekly reversal
  candle; Monday consolidation after a large Friday expansion; Tuesday reversal;
  Wednesday continuation long. Entry on the 1-minute at the break of a high with the
  stop on the 1-minute low, target just over 4R at highs to the left. Partial at 2R,
  trailed out at the same level as the take-profit; price later reached the hourly
  objective after he was flat.
- **YM / ES / NQ relative strength**, used only to choose ES over NQ for the long.

### Quotes

> "continuations are much easier"

### Open questions / ambiguities

- Consolidation versus retracement is decided by stated distrust ("I don't really
  trust this") rather than by a rule. This is the single largest gap for a detector.
- "Aggressive" move is never quantified; the only stated evidence is that a fair
  value gap was generated.
- Whether the closure through the down-close series must be a body close is not said.
- The T-spot is described as the overlap of two 0.5 levels but no rule is given for
  what to do when they do not overlap.
- The 2R threshold is implied by his behaviour and by "that would give me 2 R and
  that is fine", but is never stated as a hard minimum.
- The relative-strength ordering is given as "YM strongest, NQ weakest… or vice
  versa", i.e. the ordering is observed per day, not fixed.

## TTrades Top-Down Chart Lessons #3
(video_id: `IzOQmcgLyA0`, https://youtu.be/IzOQmcgLyA0, 461s)

### What is actually taught (detailed)

A trade review on CAD futures that begins, deliberately, on the dollar index rather
than on the traded instrument. He reads a bearish candle 2 closure on the **monthly**
dollar chart and converts it into the only question he says matters at that scale:
is price more likely to reach the previous month's high or the previous month's low.
Price is closer to the previous month's low and there are equal lows sitting there,
so the low becomes the draw.

He then re-reads the same dollar chart by phase. A high was taken out, with
expansion into the high met with expansion out of it — a reversal. He asks whether
the next leg is a new phase and answers no: it is a retracement, because price does
not expand back higher to make a higher low and then a higher high. From there he
states the structural requirement for the continuation — he does not want the prior
high taken; he wants a new lower high and a new lower low. He marks an opposing
candle that must be respected and identifies a candle 2 closure on the daily, then
drops to the hourly to confirm a change in the state of delivery inside that candle
2, reading the **bodies** to see a V-shaped reversal.

Bearish dollar becomes bullish foreign currencies, and he scans the currency
futures for the chart he likes best. Euro is "valid" into a high; pound has a candle
2 closure; but CAD futures catch his eye for the quality of the daily formation: it
took out a low, has a V-shaped reversal, and price opens, trades into a fair value
gap, and then produces a candle 2 closure. He calls this an *ideal* candle 2 closure
specifically because it also forms a protected swing. Candle 3 (the next day) is
then expected to trade higher, targeting the previous month's high with equal highs
above as a swing target.

The hourly work is about invalidation levels rather than signals. He wants the daily
opposing candle respected, marks 0.5 of it, and requires the next candle's wick to
form in the upper half of that area *and* in the upper half of the daily candle —
the T-spot. He then hunts protected swings: price reaches into a fair value gap and
closes over it, which protects the low beneath and gives him an invalidation. A
second such event gives a new, tighter invalidation. Because price opens right next
to that invalidation, he takes a positional entry into the overnight session, stop
on the protected low, targeting the high.

Management is driven by phase again. Waking into the New York session he is over 2R,
sees three candles of expansion — his cue that a new phase of price can begin —
checks the 30-minute and finds consolidation, and therefore places a take-profit at
the high of that consolidation on the reasoning that a consolidation whose high is
taken can flip phase and reverse. He exits around 2.3R. He closes by showing the
identical setup inverted on USDCAD (bearish, same candle 2 closure, same protected
swings, same 2R) and by criticising his own asset choice: GBPUSD produced a larger
expansion that day, though CAD futures had the better structure.

### Concepts introduced (speaker's own terminology)

- Bearish/bullish candle 2 closure on the monthly and daily.
- "Is price more likely to reach for its previous month's high or its previous
  month's low" as the bias question.
- Ideal candle 2 closure (a candle 2 closure that also forms a protected swing).
- Opposing candle, and 0.5 of the opposing candle.
- The T-spot (upper half of the opposing candle overlapped with the upper half of
  the daily candle).
- Protected swing / invalidation, created by a close over a fair value gap.
- Positional entry — entering at an invalidation on the open and holding overnight.
- Dollar analysis → foreign currency inversion for instrument selection.
- Three candles of expansion as the cue that a new phase of price can start.

### Rules / conditions stated (each decidable if possible)

1. A candle 2 closure on the monthly sets the monthly bias; the bias is expressed as
   which of the previous month's extremes price reaches. (Decidable.)
2. Equal lows/highs at that extreme strengthen it as the draw. (Decidable.)
3. A leg is a retracement, not a new phase, if price does not expand back to make a
   higher low and then a higher high. (Decidable.)
4. For a bearish continuation, require a new lower high and a new lower low; do not
   allow the prior high to be taken.
5. Confirm the daily candle 2 with an hourly change in the state of delivery, read
   on the bodies. (Decidable.)
6. A candle 2 closure that also forms a protected swing is "ideal". (Decidable.)
7. A protected swing is formed when price reaches into a fair value gap and closes
   back over (bullish) or below (bearish) it. (Decidable.)
8. If price opens right near an invalidation, a positional entry can be taken into
   the overnight with the stop on the protected swing.
9. Three candles of expansion signal that a new phase of price may begin — check the
   intermediate timeframe (30m) for consolidation and take profit at the
   consolidation's high. (Decidable given the count; see ambiguities.)
10. Bearish dollar ⇒ bullish foreign currency futures; on USD-quoted pairs (USDCAD)
    the setup inverts to bearish. (Decidable.)

### Examples walked through

- **DXY / dollar, monthly → daily → hourly.** Bearish monthly candle 2 closure,
  equal lows below as the draw, hourly CISD confirming inside the daily candle 2.
- **CAD futures, daily → hourly (entry).** Ideal candle 2 closure with a protected
  swing, positional entry held overnight, take-profit at the consolidation high in
  the New York session for roughly 2.3R.
- **USDCAD**, shown as the mirrored bearish version of the same trade for
  approximately the same result.
- **GBPUSD**, shown after the fact as the instrument that expanded most that day —
  a self-criticism of the asset selection, not a trade.
- **EUR and GBP futures**, glanced at and rejected in favour of CAD's structure.

### Open questions / ambiguities

- The final instrument is chosen as the one that caught his eye among several valid
  candidates; the ranking criteria beyond having better structure are not given.
- Three candles of expansion is stated once, in passing, as a phase-change cue. It

## TTrades Top-Down Chart Lessons #4
(video_id: `w-JekHg6Ldk`, https://youtu.be/w-JekHg6Ldk, 503s)

### What is actually taught (detailed)

A scalp review on ES built explicitly around relative strength and weakness across
the three index futures, and the clearest statement in the unit of the timeframe
pairing used for scalping. The draw on liquidity is equal highs; the reason for
taking the trade at all is a previous-day-low SMT.

He ranks YM, ES and NQ using three named inputs: the location of price relative to
structure, the SMT, and the candle type. NQ is closest to its previous day low, so
it shows weakness. YM has a clean bullish expansion candle; NQ's candle is bullish
but is a reversal candle rather than an expansion candle. And NQ is below the low
while ES and YM are not — the SMT. Conclusion: NQ weakest, YM strongest. The general
rule follows: long the strongest, short the weakest.

He then shows why he does neither. NQ cannot be shorted off the open because the
highs are covered in failure swings, so a short would require waiting for
manipulation into the low first. That manipulation does occur, NQ trades into and
takes its previous day low, and produces a reversal closure. If NQ is reversing off
its previous day low, the longs are in the stronger assets — but YM has already
