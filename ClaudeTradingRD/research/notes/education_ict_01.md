## "Next Day Model" The MMXM Trader (`klaiq36QnEM`, https://youtu.be/klaiq36QnEM, 816s)

### What is actually taught

**This is the headline finding of the unit.** The video is titled "Next Day Model", presents
itself as "the nmxm Traders Twitter model" (the caption's rendering of MMXM), and states five conditions — and they are *not the
same five conditions* as the later re-recording (`WwmS47Gb3M0`, "The 'Twitter Model'"), which
the library currently holds as canonical under `mmxm-twitter-model`. The five here are entirely
about **timing and trade selection**, with no price pattern in them at all: skip Mondays; New
York session only; execute on the 15-minute; use the economic calendar as the roadmap; and trade
**the day after** the anticipated higher-timeframe level is hit. The later version's five —
previous-day-extreme raid, hourly opposing fair value gap as target, 15-minute structure shift,
SMT, midnight-open side gate — share exactly one element with these, the 15-minute execution.

The mechanism is the "next day" idea: mark a daily level in advance (a daily fair value gap, a
daily order block, a daily PD array), wait for the day on which price reaches it and reacts, and
then look for the setup **the following day** during New York. Every one of the five worked
examples runs the same top-down chain: daily supplies the level and the draw, hourly supplies a
breaker / fair value gap / order block around the midnight open, and the 15-minute supplies the
entry.

Two extras are dropped in without being developed. A weekly-profile overlay — on a bullish week
he wants the daily to print open-low-high-close — and a target preference: he likes to target
"a candle with no Wick". Neither is explained.

### Concepts introduced

The MMXM Twitter model as a timing filter, skip-Monday, the day-after-the-level rule, daily →
hourly → 15-minute top-down chain, midnight open as an hourly anchor, hourly breaker, weekly
open, previous week's high/low, the open-low-high-close bullish-week shape, wickless candles as
targets, SMT at the highs, smart money reversal (named, not defined).

### Rules / conditions stated

- Skip Monday. Wait for the day the higher-timeframe level is reached; trade the day after.
- New York session only; execution on the 15-minute.
- The economic calendar is the roadmap.
- On a bullish week expect an open-low-high-close daily candle.
- A candle with no wick is a preferred target.

### Examples walked through

Five: a daily fair value gap on an unnamed index (Mon skipped, Tue reaches the level, Wed traded);
a daily order block where Thursday tests and Friday is traded, with an hourly SMT at the highs; a
NASDAQ week where Monday takes Friday's high and closes back in, setting up the next day; two
EURUSD sequences (one where New York offers nothing and he explicitly rolls to the next day); and
a GBPUSD week off a smart money reversal where the entry is a 15-minute fair value gap at the
midnight open.

### Quotes

- "this model includes skipping Mondays focusing on the New York session only"
- "trading the day after your anticipated higher time frame level is hit"
- "a candle with no Wick which I like to Target"

### Open questions / ambiguities

- The "anticipated higher time frame level" is not typed — fair value gap, order block and
  "PD array" are all used interchangeably as the level.
- What counts as a reaction off the level is judged by eye (a closure respecting it).
- No timezone or clock hours for "the New York session".
- The wickless-candle target preference is stated once and never explained.

## 15 Second Chart Example (`aDYzoae3viQ`, https://youtu.be/aDYzoae3viQ, 160s)

### What is actually taught

A short companion example to a scalping video that lives in another unit. The chain is: a London
displacement range over structure, a 15-minute fair value gap in the discount of it as the point
of interest, then a drop to the 15-second chart to wait for displacement over 15-second structure
*into* that gap, entering on the resulting 15-second fair value gap which is itself in a discount
and an OTE. Stop on the low, first partial at the near high.

The valuable part is the honest ending: the trade is **shown losing**. He takes the first partial,
moves the stop to break-even, and is stopped out. That sentence — "move your stops to break even
at least i do one scalping" (i.e. *when scalping*) — puts him in direct opposition to the CPI
trade review in the same playlist, which argues explicitly against break-even stops. The only
discriminator he offers is that this one is a scalp.

He also uses "**up until the news embargo lifts**" as the boundary separating London price action
from New York's, which pins the 8:30 marker used elsewhere in the unit to a named event.

### Concepts introduced

15-second execution, displacement range over structure, 15-minute fair value gap as POI, fair
value gap fill as a marked level, OTE + discount confluence at the entry, first partial then
break-even, the news embargo as a session boundary, a target-validity check ("we're still bearish right on the one minute time frame").

### Rules / conditions stated

- Point of interest must be in a discount for a long.
- Drop to 15 seconds only once price has reached the higher-timeframe gap.
- Require displacement over 15-second structure into the gap before entering.
- Entry on the 15-second fair value gap, in discount and OTE; stop on the low.
- First partial, then stop to break-even — stated as scalping practice.
- If the highs are not taken, the one-minute picture is still bearish.

### Quotes

- "during london up until the news embargo lifts"
- "move your stops to break even at least i do one scalping"

### Open questions / ambiguities

- "One scalping" is the auto-caption of "when scalping"; the scalp/non-scalp split is the only
  thing separating this from the anti-break-even position in `0R61Y6Pn74Q`.
- No R threshold or timeframe defines a "scalp".
- Partial sizes are never given.

## A+ ICT Entry Checklist (`lpam8F-N3EM`, https://youtu.be/lpam8F-N3EM, 350s)

### What is actually taught

The earlier recording of the checklist that the library holds from `0xygpCMwxbQ`. Here it is
framed explicitly as **"my a plus setup using the 2022 mentorship model"** — an attribution the
later recording drops entirely. The steps are recognisably the same: a liquidity grab, a market
structure shift with displacement, a fair value gap or order block positioned beyond the 0.5 fib
of the **displacement range** (premium for shorts, discount for longs), the return into it, and
solid risk-to-reward.

The substantive divergence is on step 1, and it runs the opposite way from the later version.
Here he explicitly **softens** it: "when i say liquidity grab it doesn't always mean you have to
run the lows and highs" — trading into a higher-timeframe imbalance counts as the same thing,
"a higher time frame reasoning". The later recording states the *hard* version of that same
condition: without a lower-timeframe liquidity raid before the move he is not interested,
regardless of displacement. Same step, opposite direction of travel.

Stop placement is left to discretion, with one stated preference: he dislikes stops placed all
the way at the swing low ("too much risk") and prefers the fair value gap or the candle that
created it. Exits are a three-rung partial ladder anchored to liquidity, not to R.

### Concepts introduced

The 2022 mentorship model framing, liquidity grab, HTF imbalance as a liquidity-grab substitute,
market structure shift with displacement, displacement range and its 0.5 fib, premium/discount,
unmitigated fair value gap, order block, three-partial exit ladder, discretionary stop placement.

### Rules / conditions stated

- 1. Liquidity grab (equal highs/lows run) — **or** price trading into a higher-timeframe imbalance.
- 2. Market structure shift with displacement.
- 3. Fib the displacement range; entry PD array must sit beyond the 0.5 on the correct side.
- 4. Prefer an unmitigated fair value gap or order block there.
- 5. Stop above the gap or on the creating candle — user discretion; not at the swing low.
- First partial at the nearest opposing liquidity, second at the next, third beyond.

### Examples walked through

Three, on 1-minute and 5-minute charts: equal highs run then a one-minute reversal and
displacement below the lows, entry in a premium fair value gap; a five-minute case where the
liquidity "grab" is a 15-minute fair value gap rather than a sweep; and a pre-open example around
the news embargo where the trade never reaches its first partial and is described as roughly 6R
unrealised.

### Quotes

- "my a plus setup using the 2022 mentorship model"
- "it doesn't always mean you have to run the lows and highs"
- "i don't like using stops all the way to low too much risk"

### Open questions / ambiguities

- "Solid risk to reward" is never numeric in this recording (the later corpus says 2R).
- Which of the two step-1 forms takes precedence is never addressed — and the later video
  contradicts this one on exactly that point.
- "Aggressively" / "energetically" describe displacement without any threshold.

## Balance Price Range (BPR) (`yOPESX2aWkI`, https://youtu.be/yOPESX2aWkI, 200s)

### What is actually taught

The channel's own primary statement of a concept the library previously carried mostly in a
guest's voice, and it is unusually crisp: a bearish fair value gap that overlaps a bullish fair
value gap, with the traded zone being **whichever of the two is smaller** — the intersection.
The entry is the retracement back into it. He frames the whole thing as "how to use aggressive
price action to your advantage".

Two guards are attached. First, premium/discount: he explicitly declines a second, higher BPR
that also lines up, because you do not long in a premium of the set range. Second, a
prerequisite — the aggressive move down should have run *below some sort of point of interest*,
an old low or into a fair value gap, so there is a liquidity reason to be looking long at all.

The fallback when the zone gives too much risk is characteristic of this unit: use it as
**narrative** rather than as an entry — wait for an internal low to be put in, wait for that low
to be taken, and then take the higher-timeframe gap as the long. And he closes by pointing at the
box setup as the same shape, crediting "Alex's Box video".

### Concepts introduced

BPR as the intersection of opposing fair value gaps, aggressive-price-action framing, premium/
discount guard on the zone, HTF point of interest as prerequisite, deviation from the range,
BPR as bias tool on higher timeframes, internal low as an inducement step, the Alex/box link.

### Rules / conditions stated

- Find overlapping opposing fair value gaps; the BPR is the smaller one.
- Require a prior liquidity raid / point of interest below (for longs).
- Do not take a BPR that sits in a premium when you are looking long.
- If the risk is too large, wait for an internal low to form and be taken, then enter.
- The same reading on higher timeframes gives bias rather than an entry.

### Examples walked through

A 15-minute point of interest (equal lows below a midnight low) refined on the one-minute: a
displaced move down leaving stacked gaps, met by a larger aggressive move up, giving the BPR.
Then a second case at previous lows where London reaches into the BPR and the long works.

### Quotes

- "if you combine the two whatever one is smaller there is your BPR"
- "you don't want to be longing in a premium"
- "this is also very similar to Alex's Box video"

### Open questions / ambiguities

- "Aggressive" is the load-bearing word and is unquantified.
- Whether the two gaps must be adjacent in time is not stated.
- "Too much risk" has no threshold attached.

## Box Setup — The Easiest Entry Model? (`E6bpY4dQvlE`, https://youtu.be/E6bpY4dQvlE, 402s)

### What is actually taught

The earlier recording of the box setup. The core is identical to `HFfChGFHD44`: consolidation,
aggressive move **out**, aggressive move **back in**, trade the retest — and he says outright it
is accumulation / manipulation / distribution. Three differences from the later canonical version
are worth recording.

First, **attribution**: he opens by pointing viewers at "Alex's video on this", which the later
recording does not do. Second, the **rationale differs**: here it is power-of-three only; the
later recording supplies the deeper reason (this is what forms the higher-timeframe wick), which
is absent here. Third — and this is the only genuinely new *rule* — a **timing preference the
later video does not carry**: he prefers to catch the retest early, near the aggressive move,
rather than waiting for the full retracement to the far edge of the zone. The justification is
the curve: on the side of the curve where price is still seeking liquidity, a level is more
likely to be run through than to reverse, so waiting for the perfect fill is how you get
"where you think that it is going to reverse and it just blows right through".

He also gives the higher-timeframe use plainly: run the same reading on the 4-hour or 1-hour
purely for **bias**, looking only at displacement and lack of displacement, then drop lower to
reduce risk.

### Concepts introduced

Box setup, accumulation/manipulation/distribution, consolidation range marked on bodies (wicks by
discretion), BPR inside the deviation, aggressive out / aggressive in, retest entry, buy side and
sell side of the curve, box setup as a 4H/1H bias tool, broadening formations as a related shape.

### Rules / conditions stated

- Mark the consolidation range; bodies by default, wicks by discretion.
- The aggressive move out and the aggressive move back in are the important part, not the consolidation.
- Entry on the retest of the deviated edge; stop beyond the manipulation extreme; target the other side.
- Prefer to enter early in the retracement rather than at the far edge.
- Do not look for a reversal on the side of the curve that is still seeking liquidity.
- Higher-timeframe variant: read displacement / lack of displacement on 4H or 1H for bias only.

### Examples walked through

Four, working from lower timeframes upward: two small intraday boxes; a "not super aggressive"
case used to introduce the curve argument; a 4-hour/1-hour case used for bias; and a final case
where the bodies never closed back inside the range, so the fair value gaps / BPR left by the
return leg are used as the level instead.

### Quotes

- "if you haven't watched Alex's video on this please check it out"
- "consolidation aggressive down aggressive up this is what's important"
- "I prefer to catch them right away"

### Open questions / ambiguities

- "Aggressive" unquantified in both directions, as in the later version.
- The enter-early rule depends on locating the curve, and nothing locates the curve.
- The transcript's buy-side/sell-side labelling is internally inconsistent (see
  `market-curve-side`); the intent — do not fade the direction price is currently seeking — is clear.

## Breaker Blocks Simplified (`dcIdzqD3kMU`, https://youtu.be/dcIdzqD3kMU, 496s)

### What is actually taught

**The most automation-relevant video in the unit.** The structural definition matches the later
`rXMVPUZPw4A` exactly — bearish: a high, a low, a higher high relative to that high, a lower low
relative to that low; bullish mirrors — and the block is the opposing-close candle *or series*
between the first high and the first low. Two things here are not in the later recording.

The first is the **rationale**, stated as a mechanism: if price were building an uptrend it should
make a higher low in those down-close candles and be supported; when it breaks through instead,
that support becomes resistance, which is why price should move away from the block. The second is
the reason a **series** is used rather than a single candle: the series *is* a single candle one
timeframe up ("if we go out to a 30 minute or one time frame up that is our single candle").

The third and most valuable is a **marking procedure keyed to wick size** — the closest thing the
corpus has to answering the library's biggest open question. It is a within-candle comparison, not
an absolute threshold:

- Full-bodied candle with little wick → mark the **whole body**; the half-level is the **mean
  threshold, 50% of the body**.
- Large wick with a smaller body → mark **close-to-extreme** instead (close to the low for a
  bearish block); the half-level is the **0.5 of that wick**.

Body/wick respect is stated the usual way — bodies must respect the level, wicks may dig in — and
the ideal case is named: price taps the mean threshold and closes back out of it.

### Concepts introduced

Bearish/bullish breaker structure, support-becomes-resistance rationale, block as a series, the
series-is-one-HTF-candle argument, wick-vs-body marking rule, mean threshold, 0.5 of the wick,
volume imbalance as a second level, bodies-respect / wicks-dig-in, tap-and-close-out ideal,
4-hour fair value gap and consequent encroachment as the top-down frame.

### Rules / conditions stated

- Bearish: H1, L1, then H2 > H1, then L2 < L1. Bullish mirrors.
- Block := the opposing-close candle(s) between H1 and L1; use the whole consecutive series.
- Full body / little wick → whole body + mean threshold. Large wick / small body → close-to-extreme + 0.5 of the wick.
- Bodies must not close beyond the level; wicks may.
- Ideal: a tap of the mean threshold with a close back out of it.

### Examples walked through

Three: a 15-minute bearish breaker with full bodies, using the body and mean threshold plus a
volume imbalance (bodies never close over it, wicks do); a bullish breaker reached top-down from a
4-hour fair value gap and its consequent encroachment, marked on a 30-minute up-close candle; and
a third deliberately chosen because the candle has a large wick, demonstrating the close-to-low
marking and its 0.5.

### Quotes

- "a high a low a higher high relative to this high"
- "a large Wick and smaller body I normally focus on the wick"
- "the reason for using the series and not just a single is because"
- "when price Taps the mean threshold but closes out of it"

### Open questions / ambiguities

- **Still not a number.** "Little wicks" versus "a large Wick and smaller body" is a comparison
  with no ratio. `wick_span > body_span` is the reading the words support, but he never says it.
- Which wick is measured when the candle has wicks on both sides is not stated.
- Whether the higher high / lower low must *close* beyond the prior extreme or may only wick
  beyond is not stated — the same gap the later recording has.
- The later recording narrows the block to the lowest down-close candle prior to the move (its wording, per the updated notes);
  this one says the series without that qualifier.

## Broadening Formations — Strat and ICT Part 1 (`dSccSI7uqvE`, https://youtu.be/dSccSI7uqvE, 287s)

### What is actually taught

The earlier recording, and the framing is completely different: this is announced as **"the first
video in a two-part series of combining the Strat with ICT"** — a framing that does not survive
into the later `Tjk9bXERZy0`, which reframes the same pattern as ICT Seek & Destroy. (Part 2,
"Strat Entries", sits in `education_ict_02`.)

The definition is minimal and mechanical to the point of being usable: "all a broadening formation
is is one high taking out another high", mirrored for lows, with the line drawn between the taking
extreme and the taken extreme and extended as new extremes print. The trigger for looking is a
**range-bound market**, on the reasoning that a consolidation must eventually expand the range.

The directional read matches the later version — a run of one side that fails implies rotation to
the other — but the *trade* is spelled out here in a way the later video does not: after the run,
wait for a break of structure in the new direction on a lower timeframe, take a fair value gap
that sits in the **discount of that displacement range**, stop beyond the run extreme, first
partial at the opposing high.

Absent here and present later: the outside-bar higher-timeframe identity, the two drawing methods,
the four pairings (SMT, power of three, box setup, premium/discount), and the entire Seek & Destroy
session-nesting layer.

### Concepts introduced

Broadening formation as one extreme taking another, range-bound as the trigger, consolidation
implies eventual expansion, failed side → rotation, broadening line as a point of interest,
lower-timeframe break of structure, fair value gap in the discount of the displacement range,
inducement (named in passing).

### Rules / conditions stated

- Only look for the formation once price is range-bound.
- Draw the line between the extreme that was taken and the extreme that took it; extend as new ones print.
- A failed run of one side implies rotation to the other side of the range.
- Trade it on a lower timeframe: break of structure, then a fair value gap in the discount of the displacement range.
- First partial at the opposing short-term liquidity.

### Examples walked through

Two 15-minute sequences: recent price action where highs and lows are each taken and both lines
are drawn; and a 22 May case where the upper side is run into the formation, the range gaps down,
the lower side is taken with short-term liquidity, a break of structure follows, and a fair value
gap in the discount of the displacement range gives the long.

### Quotes

- "all a broadening formation is is one high taking out another high"
- "once we get range bound that is when I start to look for broad informations"
- "since we failed this side of the range we expect to see the other side"

### Open questions / ambiguities

- "Range bound" is the entry trigger and is never bounded.
- The formations nest at every scale; no rule selects the operative one (same gap as later).
- Auto-caption mangles "broadening formation" four different ways.

## Build An Entry Model (`A8UpRZlRzlg`, https://youtu.be/A8UpRZlRzlg, 337s)

### What is actually taught

A meta-video: not a setup but a procedure for *constructing* one, and it is the clearest statement
in the unit of what his configuration parameters actually are. The construction order is stated:
(1) choose the timeframe you enter on; (2) choose **which kill zones you enter in**, and hunt the
model only inside them; (3) choose the concepts your model is allowed to use. His own answers are
given: 1-minute and 15-second execution, and "I trade the New York open".

The permitted concept vocabulary is enumerated and closed: displacement / fair value gaps, order
blocks, OTE, discount and premium, SMT, inducement, BPR. "Those are the only Concepts I use when
finding an entry."

The payoff is a compact lattice of five models, which he says are the four posted on his Twitter
plus BPR, and which is fully recoverable from two "only difference" sentences:

- **Model 1** — liquidity grab → break of structure with displacement → entry on the retracement into the resulting fair value gap.
- **Model 2** — model 1, plus internal range liquidity taken before the expansion leg.
- **Model 3** — model 1, plus the entry must sit in an OTE of the displacement leg. Added purely for risk-to-reward.
- **Model 4** — model 3 plus the internal-range-liquidity requirement.
- **Model 5 (BPR)** — aggressive buy met with aggressive sell (or the reverse); enter at the overlap of the two gaps.

The closing instruction is the point of the video: draw the diagram, label it, and stick to it.

### Concepts introduced

Entry-timeframe choice, kill-zone choice as a model parameter, the closed concept vocabulary, the
four numbered models, OTE as a risk-to-reward add-on rather than a signal, internal range liquidity
as a model variant, BPR model, diagram-and-stick-to-it discipline.

### Rules / conditions stated

- Choose the execution timeframe first, then the kill zone, then the concept set.
- Search for the model only inside the chosen kill zone.
- Model 1/3 differ only by the OTE requirement; models 2/4 add internal range liquidity.
- BPR entry is at the overlap of the two opposing fair value gaps.
- Pick one model, diagram it, and trade it consistently.

### Quotes

- "the next thing you need to know is which kill zones you enter in"
- "the only difference between model one and model 3"
- "there is internal range liquidity being taken before they move higher or lower"
- "BPR which is really just an aggressive buy met with an aggressive sell"

### Open questions / ambiguities

- The models are numbered against on-screen diagrams; the numbering survives only because of the
  two "only difference" sentences.
- The kill-zone gate is asserted as mandatory but **no hours are given** — only "I trade the New York open".
- No rule says which of the five to use on a given day.
- Higher-timeframe points of interest are explicitly declared out of scope.

## CPI Gold Trade Review | ICT Market Maker Sell Model (`0R61Y6Pn74Q`, https://youtu.be/0R61Y6Pn74Q, 508s)

### What is actually taught

A full trade review, and the densest video in the unit for *rules he does not state anywhere else*.

**Calendar as a participation requirement, not an avoidance filter.** He walks the week's economic
calendar on screen and declines Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday *because they carry no news at all*,
waiting for Thursday, "our first red folder News day of the week", and placing his interest
specifically **post 8:30**. This is a stronger use of the calendar than anything in the later corpus,
which treats it mainly as something to avoid around.

**Failure swings and resistance grading.** A day whose high is lower than the previous day's high
"creates a failure swing or low resistance liquidity". The positioning rule he derives is a pairing:
trading lower, he wants **high** resistance liquidity above (protecting the upside) and targets
**low** resistance liquidity below — and he explicitly refuses to short into failure-swing highs,
waiting for an actual sweep.

**Advanced structure used as a stop-placement device.** The arithmetic is stated cleanly: a high
higher than the short-term highs on both sides of it is an **intermediate-term high**. In real time
that means: sweep a high, then wait for a short-term high to print on the far side; only then is the
swept high confirmed as intermediate-term and usable as the stop. The confirming short-term high is

## Choosing Dealing Ranges & Displacement Ranges (`CAuN4tvLInQ`, https://youtu.be/CAuN4tvLInQ, 351s)

### What is actually taught

A definitional video that separates two objects the corpus elsewhere blurs.

A **dealing range** is the range price is currently trading inside, and his method for finding it is
deliberately naive: zoom in, look at where price actually is, take that high and that low, fib
between them. The consequence he draws is the useful one — dealing ranges **nest**, and "as you zoom
out that external liquidity becomes internal liquidity", so there is no single correct range, only
the one appropriate to where price is now. A range whose extremes price has already left behind is
discarded and a wider pair taken.

A **displacement range** is different: "displacement ranges are aggressive price action moves over
structure". Its anchors are stated precisely — the low that *started* the move and that *broke the
structure*, up to the high the move reaches — and the high anchor is **trailed upward** as the move
extends. What he wants next is a retracement into the discount of that leg before continuation.

The best case is when the two agree: a level in the discount of the displacement range *and* the
discount of the dealing range *and* on a PD array (an order block, in his example) — at which point
he shows candles refusing to close through the block and the move resuming. He then repeats the
whole exercise for a second entry, reading 0.62 off the fib.

This video also contains **the only explicit clock reading in the unit**: the New York morning
session "is right about here 8 30 start and then we get into 9 30".

### Concepts introduced

Dealing range, nesting of external/internal liquidity, displacement range and its anchoring rule,
trailing the displacement anchor, discount of the displacement range, OTE, PD arrays inside points
of interest, the 8:30/9:30 New York morning markers, order block bodies-respect.

### Rules / conditions stated

- Dealing range := nearest high and nearest low bounding current price at your zoom level.
- If price has left the range, redraw wider.
- Displacement range := aggressive move over structure, anchored at the low that broke the structure; trail the far anchor.
- Require a retracement into the discount of the displacement leg before continuation.
- Best entries sit in the discount of both ranges and on a PD array.

### Quotes

- "here's a high here's a low that is your dealing range"
- "displacement ranges are aggressive price action moves over structure"
- "I take the low that started this displacement range which broke this structure"
- "which is right about here 8 30 start and then we get into 9 30"

### Open questions / ambiguities

- "Zoom your screen in" is a UI instruction, not a bar-count — two traders at different zooms get
  different ranges. Decidable only once the zoom is fixed.
- "aggressive price action moves over structure" leaves which legs qualify to judgement.
- The trailing of the displacement anchor has no stop condition.
- 8:30 and 9:30 are given with **no end time and no timezone**.

## Finding Entries Into Trend (`NgFIza9qsGQ`, https://youtu.be/NgFIza9qsGQ, 584s)

### What is actually taught

**The second most automation-relevant video in the unit**, and the only one with a clean
transcription (it is the one video here with punctuated captions).

It opens with **his own definition of trend**, which differs from every other definition in the
corpus: "the way I define trend is an aggressive move reaching towards an objective" — the
objective being a marked higher-timeframe point of interest such as a 4-hour or daily order block.
The corollary is stated immediately: in a real trend you do not want many pullbacks, which is
exactly why the normal retracement entries are unavailable. He makes the consequence explicit —
he will not take entries directly off fair value gaps in a trend because the stops are too wide,
and cites the arithmetic (a 4-point stop is a lot more preferred than a 16-point stop).

Then an enumerated menu: **"There are four ways I look to enter trend."**

1. **Inducement** — stops raided beyond a swing, price returns into the range, enter on the return.
   With a confident variant: **turtle soup**, shorting *above* the high as it is raided with a fixed stop.
2. **Mitigation block** — in a bearish trend, an up-close candle whose **low is broken** becomes a
   mitigation block, and its **high must not be invalidated**; enter on the retest of that high or of
   the candle's opening price, stop just beyond.
3. **Cheat code** — credited by name to "my friend Austin". If you already expect the candle to
   become a mitigation block, do not wait for the break: when the opposing candle **closes**, enter at
   the **open of the very next candle**, stop **one point** beyond the signal candle's extreme.
4. **Lower-timeframe model inside the mitigation block** — drop to a 15- or 30-second chart and take
   a fair value gap or box setup inside the block.

A fifth (fair value gaps) is added mid-video as an afterthought and immediately de-rated, which
makes the count of four unstable.

The other durable contribution is a **second wick-size rule**, given for marking mitigation blocks:
"very small body, and I'll use the wicks... When price is more clustered together here, I will use
the opening price." It is a different test from the breaker-block one — the second half is about the
neighbourhood of candles, not the candle itself.

Trend as aggressive move to an objective, four trend-entry options, inducement entry, turtle soup

stop-width arithmetic, failure to displace as the trend-continuation tell.

### Rules / conditions stated

- Trend := aggressive move reaching toward a marked objective; few pullbacks expected.
- Do not enter directly off a fair value gap while trending — drop timeframes instead.
- Inducement: raid, return into range, enter; turtle soup variant enters into the raid with a fixed stop.
- Mitigation block: opposing candle's extreme broken → its far extreme is the invalidation; enter on the retest of the extreme or the opening price.
- Very small body → use the wicks; clustered candles → use the opening price.
- Cheat code: enter at the open of the candle after the opposing candle closes; stop one point beyond.
- Fourth option: 15s/30s fair value gap or box setup inside the mitigation block.

### Examples walked through

Four sequences on the same instrument: an inducement short at a high that failed to displace, with

## ICT Daily Bias (`tZMgGsTqqVM`, https://youtu.be/tZMgGsTqqVM, 348s)

### What is actually taught

**The earliest bias procedure in the corpus**, and structurally different from everything that
follows. It is a five-item ordered read, each item with a stated reason:

1. **Previous liquidity taken** — because liquidity accumulated at a low must be distributed higher,
   and vice versa. This rationale is the origin of the accumulation/distribution language he uses later.
2. **Swing highs and lows, short-term highs and lows, relatively equal highs and lows** — marked
   because that is where stops rest.
3. **Market structure shifts and imbalances** — read as the change in trend.
4. **Equilibrium / premium / discount** — because liquidity taken low should be distributed at a premium.
5. The plain lower-high / lower-low sequence, to see which way price is turning.

He then cleans the chart down to the closest imbalances and liquidity and states the bias — and the
important part is that **the bias is a path, not a direction**. He is bullish overall to equal highs
around 4300, but wants a move *down* to take resting liquidity first, and so states that he is
**bearish on the daily and lower timeframes until that liquidity is grabbed, then bullish**. The
conservative alternative is offered in the same breath: just wait for the grab and trade the second leg.

The closing claim is that this style is self-protecting: "even if your bias is wrong" you are still
trading to and from liquidity, so you are kept out of trades you should not be in.

The video is explicitly rushed — recorded quickly before a trip — and it shows in the structure.

Five-item bias framework, accumulate-then-distribute rationale, relatively equal highs/lows as stop
pools, market structure shift + imbalance as trend change, premium/discount for distribution,
split bias across timeframes, path-not-direction bias, chart cleanup to closest levels, wrong-bias
tolerance.

### Rules / conditions stated

- Read the five items in order; reduce to the closest imbalance and closest liquidity.
- Liquidity taken at a low is distributed higher; taken at a high, distributed lower.
- State the bias as a path through the intervening liquidity, not as a single direction.
- Run the opposite bias on lower timeframes until the intervening liquidity is taken, then flip.
- Conservative variant: skip the first leg, wait for the grab.

### Quotes

- "the first thing i look at is previous liquidity taken"

## Improve Your Trade Entries Within Fair Value Gaps (`fwUUkJ5q6tc`, https://youtu.be/fwUUkJ5q6tc, 145s)

### What is actually taught

1. Put a **0.5 fib** on the gap and enter at its midpoint rather than at its far edge.
2. **Look left** for an order block or breaker that overlaps the gap, and enter at the **open** of

   — a daily gap refined by an hourly order block; a 5-minute gap refined by a 1-minute order block.

The trade-off is stated rather than hidden: "you might not catch every play but you reduce your
risk and it allows you to have a lower win percentage". He also notes the methods **converge** —
in his last example the 1-minute order block and the 0.5 fib land in nearly the same place, which
is the closest thing to a validation offered.

### Concepts introduced

Fair value gap entry refinement, 0.5 of the gap (consequent encroachment by another name), order
block / breaker overlap, order block **open** as the entry price, nested lower-timeframe imbalance,
risk reduction versus fill probability, convergence of refinement methods.

### Rules / conditions stated

- Enter at the 0.5 of the gap rather than at its far edge.
- Look left for an overlapping order block or breaker; use its opening price.
- Refine a large gap with a smaller imbalance one or more timeframes down.
- Accept missed entries as the cost of the smaller stop.

### Quotes

- "just to throw a 0.5 bib on there and your entry would be here"
- "using the open of that order block you can refine your entry there"
- "it allows you to have a lower win percentage"

### Open questions / ambiguities

- No rule when the three refinements disagree.
- "Look to your left" has no lookback bound.
- How far to drop the timeframe is shown, not stated.
- The lower-win-percentage claim is asserted, never quantified.

## Cross-video observations

### 1. This unit is the corpus's best source on wick size — and still stops short of a number

Two independent procedures keyed to wick size appear here and nowhere else, both of them
**within-candle comparisons rather than absolute thresholds**:

- `dcIdzqD3kMU`: a full-bodied candle with little wick → mark the **whole body**, half-level = the
  **mean threshold (50% of the body)**. A candle with a **large wick and a smaller body** → mark
  **close-to-extreme**, half-level = **0.5 of that wick**.
- `NgFIza9qsGQ`: a **very small body** → use the **wicks**; candles **clustered together** → use the
  **opening price**.

This is the first evidence in the library that the wick/body question has an operational answer at
all, and it settles *what changes* when a wick is large — which level you mark and which half-level
you measure. It does **not** settle *how large is large*. `wick_span > body_span` is the reading his
words support, and a detector has to pick that or something like it, but he never says it.

Note carefully that this is a **different question** from the one the expansion-candle concepts ask.
Those need to know whether a wick is small enough for the candle to count as an expansion candle;
this unit answers, for a given candle, which of its levels to mark. Do not conflate them.

### 2. "Aggressive" is used more heavily here than anywhere and quantified nowhere

The word carries the box setup (out and back in), the BPR (buy met with sell), the displacement
range (moves over structure), the market structure shift, and — new in this unit — **the definition
of trend itself**. `trend-as-aggressive-move-to-objective` is filed `underspecified` for exactly
this reason. The nearest thing to a proxy anywhere in the unit is comparative rather than absolute:
in `xvqsLTpmpsI` he rejects a candidate by holding its displacement range against the previous one
("look at this displacement range versus this"). That comparison is implementable; the absolute is not.

### 3. Session hours: a partial answer, and a confirmation that the gate is configurable

`A8UpRZlRzlg` states the kill zone as a **required configuration parameter of any entry model** —
second only to choosing the execution timeframe — which is the strongest statement in the corpus
that the kill-zone gate is real. But the same video gives no hours, only "I trade the New York open".
The only clock reading in the entire unit is `CAuN4tvLInQ`: the New York morning session "8 30 start
and then we get into 9 30", with **no end time and no timezone**, plus `aDYzoae3viQ`'s framing of
8:30 as the moment "the news embargo lifts". This is consistent with the library's existing indices
window (NY AM 08:30–11:00) but does not independently establish it.
